§1 — Verdict

Over the fortnight from 12 to 26 September the crew pattern — coordinators running /reckon-build and dispatching every node to a worker on the local lane or a codex lane — landed 2,303 runs across five repositories, three times August's daily rate, and the work it landed is well verified: independent reviews catch real defects, negative controls catch tests that cannot fail, and fewer than 3% of product lines are deleted within a week. It is also heavier and slower per node than it needs to be, and the weight is growing faster than the output.

  • Velocity. A node takes a median 44 minutes to complete and 83 to promote, against 9.8 minutes per run in August. When review became mandatory on 21 September, the review share of promotions went from 25% to 57%, promotions rose 77% and source lines landed fell 27% (§4).
  • Where the time goes. Serial reviews with one repair node per finding, a local lane run at twice its generation width, merge conflicts in record files every node shares (38% of documentation merges conflict), and refusals — a third of all dispatch and promotion attempts are refused. 44% of worker file edits are record-keeping, and a coordinator spends 21.6 responses on each promoted run (§5).
  • Quality. Better-verified, not better-designed: each node is locally correct, and nothing owns the codebase between nodes (§6).
  • Code. Exclusive write scopes make a private copy the cheapest compliant move: 44 timestamp parsers and 14 atomic JSON writers in reckon, and a crew interface that grew 226% in three weeks (§7).
  • nova. No broad rise in flapping, and no lane difference in churn. Readiness did go backwards, through the process: the default test command stopped at collection and ran no test from 29 August to at least 19 September while nodes promoted on their own targeted gates; lint findings doubled; nine commits in ten touched no code in the latest week; and 103 of 348 fleet-made figures misrepresent their physics (§8).
  • Weight. 49,000 words of binding instruction before a coordinator reads a plan; reckon opened 58 plans in the fortnight and closed 7; half the fleet's promotions are reckon working on itself (§9).

The remedy is to take ceremony away where it does not pay, not to add more: size review to risk, give each node its own record, refuse only defects, make a small change a small node, measure velocity continuously, give shared primitives one home, cut the build skill to a core, and cap open work. Those are the eight plans of sprint S25 (§12). The five coordinators surveyed agree on almost all of it (§3).

§2 — Method

Two evidence streams, deliberately independent. A survey of every live coordinator session on the workstation that was not itself a crew worker: five sessions, nine questions, answered in writing within forty minutes and reproduced verbatim in §13. Six measured studies, dispatched as crew nodes under orchestrator-crew-pattern-studies — three on the codex lane (velocity, code depth and reuse, nova history) and three on the local lane (worker anatomy, coordinator overhead, nova readiness), of which the last two spent their fences in the local lane's queue and were moved to codex to finish, and the readiness study completed only partially — each writing a census script that reproduces its compact output byte for byte, over the closed window 2026-09-12T00:00Z to 2026-09-26T10:00Z so that growing ledgers cannot move the counts. The August figures come from the return-on-experience review and are quoted, not re-derived. The coordinator added its own readings of the protocol's size over time, the plan backlog, the live fleet and the local lane's router gate, each timestamped where it appears.

Running the studies through the pattern under review was part of the method, and several of its findings were first observed there: the six nodes' shared default scope, the goal lint's refusal of "TODO", the review reflex racing a coordinator's correction, and census workers committing 4 to 17 MB of JSON because the brief set no size bound — the coordinator's defect, corrected by resuming each worker. Figures in this record are readings with a timestamp, not constants: re-derive before quoting.

Between workers

Wall clock between dispatch and promotion is dominated by three waits that are not work. The local lane is driven at about twice its width: at both readings taken during this review the router gate held 14 requests in flight with 11 and then 15 queued, and the three local-lane studies ran 95 minutes and more against 55-minute budgets, with their streams idle for up to three minutes between events. Review is serial after the run it reviews, and since 21 September there has been one for every finished run (§4). And promotion waits on the coordinator: median 44 minutes from dispatch to worker completion becomes 83 minutes to promotion.

§6 — Does it produce better work?

It produces better-verified work, not better-designed work. Four independent readings agree on the first half.

  • Every surveyed coordinator named defects an independent review caught that it would otherwise have shipped (§3). Across 679 distinct review records to 25 September, 86% carried at least one finding and 12.9% of source-change reviews led to an observable repair (review-node utility).
  • Mature product lines survive: under 3% were deleted within a week (§4).
  • The negative control and the integrated-gate re-run are the two ceremonies every coordinator would keep: each caught tests that could not fail.
  • In nova, independent audits found the local lane's defect rate indistinguishable from the metered lanes' on the same classes of work (§8).

The second half is where the evidence turns. The gate a worker writes and runs on its own diff passes between 93% and 100% of the time on every configuration with a real sample, so it carries almost no information (dispatch-cost measurement §4). A day's forty merged code commits read against their briefs found eleven that met the brief in letter only (brief conformance). The review's measured repair yield is one in eight for source and one in forty for documents. And the design-level defects this review found — forty-four timestamp parsers, fourteen atomic writers, a reckon interface that grew 83% in three weeks (§7); 103 nova figures that misrepresent their physics (§8) — are exactly the kind that no per-node gate or per-diff review can see, because each node's diff is locally correct. The pattern verifies nodes; nothing in it owns the codebase. The plainest instance is nova: its default test command stopped at collection and ran no test from 29 August to at least 19 September, and nodes kept promoting throughout, each on the targeted tests its own brief named (§8). The integrated-gate re-run checks a run's own gate at the merged head; nothing checks the project's.

reckon is not shallow in the pass-through sense, and it is getting wider much faster than it is getting deeper. Pure forwarding functions are rare (0.8% of reckon's functions) and function lengths are ordinary. What moved is the interface: reckon's public definitions grew 83% in three weeks and the crew package's 226%; the CLI grew from 202 to 239 option occurrences and 96 to 112 distinct flags, the MCP surface from 26 to 30 tool-view pairs, and the dispatch refusal families from none to 23. That is the opposite of a small-interface pattern.

Reimplementation is real and concentrated in exactly the places the crew touches most. The census found 44 functions in reckon that parse an ISO timestamp themselves — 27 of them in the crew package, with names like _parse_stamp (five modules), _moment, _as_moment, _observed_moment, _normalize_stamp and _parse_utc_timestamp. Fourteen functions write JSON atomically, each with its own temporary naming, cleanup and fsync behaviour. Three enumerate a run's original and resumed streams, so billing and promotion can disagree about which attempts exist. Four byte-identical numeric decoders carry four names. Clone share in the crew package rose from 5.5% to 7.1% while its code lines grew 2.6-fold (its source lines tripled), so duplicated crew lines grew from 563 to 1,885. The ten ranked consolidation candidates, with every copy's file:line and a proposed single home, are in the census findings.

The dispatch pattern produces this, mechanically. Almost every copy above is a helper inside the module the node was allowed to write, and most are private (a leading underscore). A worker holds exclusive write paths and is told not to touch anything else; when it needs a timestamp parser and the shared one is outside its fence — or does not exist yet — the cheapest compliant move is a private copy. Nothing downstream notices: the gate tests the node's behaviour, the review reads the node's diff, and neither compares the new helper against the other forty-three. The prior-art scout rule exists (reckon-build hard rule 17) but it is prose, applied per wave, and it cannot grant a node the write path to the shared home it names. Isolation made the work verifiable and made the codebase repeat itself.

nova's picture is different in kind. Its public surface barely moved (+5.7%) and its clone share rose less than a point. The duplicated concepts there are scientific algebra — seven constraint residual methods with identical bodies and five identical dual-flux-image methods in nova/equilibrium/constraint.py, three uniform-axis validators, three immutable-array constructors, three atomic receipt writers — the ordinary debt of a large numerical code rather than an accelerating pattern.

§8 — nova: flapping, readiness and evidence

In short: the lead's sense that nova went backwards is right, but not for the reason suspected. Code did not flap more under the local lane. Readiness regressed through the process: the default test command could not run for four weeks while nodes promoted on their own targeted gates, lint findings doubled, and nearly a third of the fleet's nova figures misrepresent their physics.

The lead's impression is that recent local-lane work in nova has been flapping — rewriting the same code back and forth — so that the solver's overall quality and readiness have gone backwards. Three instruments bear on it: the history census (study §6), the readiness snapshots (study §7), and nova's own figure-and-solver audit, whose independent reviewers scored every figure the fleet had produced.

Code flapping: localised, not a broad rise, and not specific to the local lane

Across additions with a full seven-day follow-up, source churn was 5,474 of 59,037 lines (9.27%) before 12 September and 694 of 7,390 (9.39%) after; test churn 2.79% and 3.10%. Over the same boundary the local lane's share of nova's code landings rose from 7.7% to 65.1%. Splitting at the local lane's first code landing (5 September) instead moves source churn down, from 10.86% to 6.58%. By lane, mature source churn is 9.73% for codex, 8.80% for local and 14.77% for claude. There is no aggregate signal that the local lane made nova churn more.

Reversals do exist, and reading them as code is what distinguishes them. Of the five worst histories: a compiled-slice cache experiment in reduced_newton.py returned the 116 KB file byte for byte to its starting state after five withdrawals (codex-terra, caught on the worker branch before merge); a certificate test was made permissive by one metered run (codex-luna) and restored by a local run; a playable-session expectation went True → False → True across one local and two codex runs, each swing tracking a real design change; a request dispatcher was added and removed 44 hours later, both by local runs — the clearest local-only rework; and transport-geometry prototypes were rewritten three times and retired, 1,327 lines deleted within a week, all codex. Four hot files — fixed_point.py, forward.py, forward_operator.py, topology.py — changed in every one of the six weeks, and 95 functions changed in three or more.

Where nova's effort went instead

The stronger signal is not reversal but displacement. In the week beginning 19 September nova landed 872 first-parent commits and only 95 of them touched nova/ or tests/; source additions fell from about 19,000 a week at the start of September to 4,314. Across the window 2,434 of 3,879 first-parent commits (63%) carry no crew attribution and are predominantly record and coordinator commits. nova's attempts per landed node (2.23) is the highest of the five projects.

And the evidence the fleet produced about the solver is weak. nova's figure-and-solver audit had claude-opus reviewers, independent of the producing lanes, open all 348 figures in the fleet's figure directories: mean score 55.7 of 100, 242 under 70, and 103 misrepresenting their physics — most often a state that did not converge drawn and captioned as a converged terminal state, and numbers printed that appear in no receipt. On the classes where both lanes have audited promotions, the audit found the lane was not the variable: solver changes were defective in 9 of 11 local-lane and 5 of 6 metered-lane promotions reviewed. Its conclusion — that the promotion process, not the model, is the lever — is the same one this review reaches from the other direction.

The protocol. A coordinator starts every session with about 49,000 words of binding instruction before it reads a plan — the build skill, its references and the global guardrails — and each of those documents records measured incidents as rules. Each rule is individually justified; together they are a document no session reads end to end, which is why the survey's complaints are about rules colliding (a goal lint refusing a semicolon, a review reflex racing a coordinator's repair) rather than about rules missing.

The output. The fleet adds 5.35 lines of plan, evidence, research and state for every product line, before counting figure data. In reckon the HTML record alone is 1.9 times the source. This review's own six study nodes reproduced it: each census worker, asked for a data directory, also wrote a render script, a verifier, an evidence anchor, a plan comment and a figure, and three of them committed between 4 MB and 17 MB of JSON — 451,454 lines in one case — before being resumed to move it out of the repository.

The friction. Dispatching those six nodes required a committed plan of a type that can carry nodes (a research record cannot), cleared nine audit warnings, one goal-lint refusal of the word "TODO" inside a done-when, a backend switch because the codex lane's 25-minute ceiling is below a census's needs, and a port override to push from a compute node. After dispatch, the default scope widened every one of the six nodes to the same plan file, the same cumulative evidence record and the same figure directory — the precise conflict the survey ranks first — and each completed run's review was dispatched by the reflex before the coordinator could correct it, then demanded again at the corrected head. Integrating the first three studies took two add/add conflicts on the shared evidence record, a fast-forward refused because main had moved, three refused promotions — a review keyed to the pre-correction head, a live peer worker's uncommitted edit to the shared record, a missing gate-log path — and four promotion waivers. Two of the three local-lane studies spent their whole 55-minute fence queueing and were moved to codex to finish. The read-back reviews of the eight new plans were first refused because one run was waiting on its own in-flight review, and four then died at launch on a 503 from the local lane's model catalog; the discarded runs left their worktrees behind, so their node ids could not be reused.

One checkout, many writers. Every coordinator merges and promotes in the same main checkout. While this review was being integrated, its git add of the review record landed inside another coordinator's unresolved merge and was committed as part of that merge (6949a0e9, now annotated with a git note); a peer reports this as the second such cross-session case that day, the first being a promotion committing into an open merge. The repair — refusing any crew git write while a merge is open in the shared checkout — is already recorded against a-crew-process-ends-with-its-owner. The same checkout is also the installed package: at 14:42Z this session's follower died at start-up on a flight-config validation error because it loaded while a peer's merge had written the new configuration key but not yet its schema, so for that window every crew command on the workstation would have failed the same way.

Read-backs work, and cost. The rubber-duck read-back each new plan must pass before release found between 8 and 29 sentences with two readings in each of the eight S25 plans, and re-checking the figures they cited surfaced five errors in this review's own numbers — the plan cohort, the crew package's growth, the second week's promotion rise, the reckon interface's growth, and a claim that every duplicated helper is private — all corrected before release. Each read-back took 60 to 90 minutes on the queued local lane.

Who writes the memory. During this review three entries appeared in the reckon project's persistent Claude memory, written by worker sessions rather than any coordinator. They were accurate, but the store is shared by every session in the repository, unreviewed, and outside version control — the property the global guardrails already warn about.

The backlog. reckon opened 58 plans between 12 and 26 September and, as of the end of that window, had closed 7 of them (two more sit at impl 1.0 with an active status); the four August weeks closed about nine in ten of what they opened. 62 reckon plans are pending, most of them about the crew itself. A system whose largest customer is itself, and which answers every measured defect with another plan, will keep growing whether or not it gets better.

§10 — The lead's questions, answered

Does the pattern result in better work and higher throughput?
Better-verified work, yes; better-designed work, no (§6, §7). Throughput in volume, yes — three times August's promotions per day — but each node takes four to five times as long to land, the second week raised its node count 77% while landing 27% less source, and half the fleet's output is reckon building reckon (§4).
What are the bottlenecks?
In order of measured cost: the serial review of every run and its one-node-per-finding repairs; the local lane driven at twice its generation width; merge conflicts in shared record files; refusals over wording and bookkeeping; and the coordinator's promotion beat. Model quality is not on the list: on the classes where both lanes have audited work, the lane is not the variable (§5, §8).
Have we designed a system that is too heavy?
Yes. The coordinator's binding instructions tripled, the crew interface more than tripled, 44% of worker edits are record-keeping, and reckon opened eight plans for every one it closed in the fortnight (§9). Most of the weight is individually justified; the defect is that nothing ever removes any.
Can we relax the need for a plan before a worker is dispatched?
Keep the attachment to a plan and section — it is where impl, followups and evidence resolve, and the survey wants it kept — but drop the authoring and commit of new plan text for follow-ups, audits and review-finding repairs. A finding or a followup should dispatch as a brief (a-small-change-is-a-small-node, on top of the stored-brief carrier a-worker-can-take-a-brief already building).
Are we passing too much context to workers?
Not in the brief. A local worker's first request is about 65,000 tokens, far inside the lane's window, and no lane refused one. The leak that mattered — one task's session inherited by another — closed on 23 September. What costs workers is not what they are told but what they are made to write: two edits in five go to the record (§5).
Should tasks be split smaller?
No. A node's cost is a fixed floor, so splitting fixed work into k nodes multiplies its cost by about k (dispatch-cost measurement §3), and each node now pays a review and a promotion wait. Single-deliverable nodes of 30–60 minutes land; what should change is to batch a review's findings into one repair instead of one node each, and to stop sizing a small node's ceremony like a feature's.
Are the orchestrators doing too much or too little?
Too much bookkeeping and too little ownership. A coordinator spends 21.6 responses and about twenty tool calls on each promoted run — more on git and merges (3.1) and on reading diffs and logs (2.7) than on dispatching (1.6) — and a third of its dispatch and promotion attempts are refused (§5). They spend their turns on promotion beats, conflict unions and refusals; nobody owns the codebase between nodes, which is why a pattern that verifies each node still reimplements the same helper forty-four times (§7). The first half is fixed by the S25 plans; the second needs a shared-primitive home and a copy detector at promotion rather than more coordinator reading.
Should an orchestrator implement small tasks directly?
For records, yes; for product code, no. A coordinator should commit, from a declared list, the edits that are bookkeeping — a mistyped sha, a heading, the union of an HTML or JSON conflict, a manifest path — as s22 and s23 already do. Product code stays delegated: the survey's majority holds that the worktree, gate and negative control are what keep the evidence honest, and the one coordinator that edited product code under pressure caused an outage. The honest answer to "a 20-minute edit took 2.5 hours" is to make a small node cost 20 minutes, not to route around the node.

Eight plans, in sprint S25 — A lighter crew, sequenced so that the changes that return wall clock soonest go first and the rewrites that must describe settled rules go last. nova's share is a section, not a new plan. nova already has an active plan for exactly this — nova-portfolio-replan, in its active sprint S21, evaluating every pending plan against the code and naming thin-interface refactors — whose nodes never ran because the local lane took over an hour each. Adding a tenth plan to a project with eight open sprints would repeat the problem this review measures.

Two decisions are the lead's. open-work-is-capped carries the limit's enforcement mode and value as open decisions, because the repository holds any tightening of plan creation pending the lead's review. And S25 sits beyond the three-sprint schedule horizon while S21 to S24 hold open work, so the roadmap reads it as deferred; it has been pushed as the sprint the project schedules next, and /reckon-build S25 runs it explicitly.

Moving work off the local lane: the lane's defect rate matches the metered lanes where both were audited, and its cost is queueing, which the first two waves reduce by removing nodes rather than by rerouting them. Smaller nodes: cost per node is a fixed floor. Coordinators writing product code: the survey's majority and one outage argue against it. A flapping guard for nova: the history census found no broad rise to guard against.
