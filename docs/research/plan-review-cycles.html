Research input

Why plan-review re-review cycles do not converge

Three hundred stored reviews over thirty-one plans, with 269 consecutive review pairs where both reviewed blobs were recovered, show that the re-review loop is carried by a handful of chains — two plans account for 163 of the 300 reviews — that roughly one re-review in six is triggered by a change confined to the document unit (comments, followups, decisions), and that a fifth of the findings in a re-review land on plan text that did not change since the previous review. Replaying five damping mechanisms over the same timeline shows the cost of damping is not the reviews saved but the gating force of acted findings that stop being re-checked.

Method and sample

Everything below is recomputed by docs/figures/plan-review-cycles/measure_review_cycles.py, which reads three stores and modifies nothing: the review store under ~/.config/reckon/crew/reviews/, each project's committed crew ledger (docs/state/<project>/crew.json), and the plan HTML itself through git. For every stored review the script resolves the reviewed blob two ways: directly from the recorded reviewed_blob_sha through a batched git cat-file --batch, and, where that sha is absent, from the plan file's commit history by picking the newest commit at or before the review timestamp. All 300 blobs (100 %) were recovered from the recorded sha; no review needed the commit fallback and none was unrecovered. Snapshotted 2026-10-06T04:18Z — the store is live and was growing during the measurement (206 records at 03:36Z, 300 at 04:18Z), so every count here is a point-in-time figure.

Units are the plan's own authored record ids: one unit per section record plus _document for the scalars, decisions, followups and authored comments, exactly the keying the plan fingerprint uses. A unit “changed” between two reviews when its digest differs between the two reviewed blobs, computed with the live module's own _digest_state and _section_digests. Everything the review gate itself ignores — server-managed metadata scalars, derived scalars, run comments and section declarations — is ignored here too.

§1 — Cadence of re-review chains

Twelve plans carry three or more reviews; the review store totals 300 reviews over 31 plans, of which 16 carry no findings at all and 246 of 300 responses are recorded as acted (54 declined). The chains are extremely concentrated: a-plan-is-reviewed-before-it-is-built alone carries 102 reviews in 32.7 hours (3.1/h) and jt60sa-discovery-completion 62 in 21.3 hours (2.9/h) — together 55 % of all reviews. The median interval between consecutive reviews on the longest chain is 0.0 minutes: reviews arrive in bursts far faster than any authoring loop, which is the settle-window sweep re-reviewing each new content state, not a human cadence. The median gap from an acted response to the next review is tens of minutes (46.5 min on the longest chain), so the loop is not driven by response latency.

§2 — What changed between consecutive reviews

Of 269 consecutive pairs with both blobs in hand, 45 (16.7 %) changed only the document unit — no authored section changed at all. The document-unit moves break down as 38 comments, 8 followups and 3 decisions (a pair may move more than one key). In other words roughly one in six re-reviews was triggered by activity that a reader would call bookkeeping: a run comment landing, a followup being written or resolved. That churn is concentrated: nova:converged-forward-solve alone contributes 26 of the 31 document-only pairs, where the plan's own comments were being written between reviews. The remaining 224 pairs changed at least one authored section alongside.

§3 — Acted-on content versus fresh readings

Each finding of a re-review is placed in the authored unit its anchor points at, then classed as acted (its unit changed since the previous review, so acting on the plan plausibly produced the finding) or fresh (its unit is byte-identical to the previous review's, so the finding is a new reading of unchanged text). Of 492 findings in re-reviews, 268 (54 %) land in a changed unit and 126 (26 %) in unchanged text. Findings anchor to code as often as to plan text: 63 resolve by a section anchor (#s…), 144 by a code path the finding names appearing in exactly one section's prose, and 187 by a code path appearing in more than one section (attributed to the first candidate, so treat those as approximate). The remaining 98 (20 %) are unclassifiable — they name no section and no code path the prose mentions, and are reported as such rather than guessed. The per-plan split shows the two regimes clearly: the long chains are dominated by acted findings (141 of 183 on the longest chain), while short chains such as nova:converged-forward-solve and forward-solver-route-integrity are dominated by fresh readings of unchanged text — the pattern of a reviewer re-reading a plan nobody edited.

§4 — Instance-at-a-time findings

Grouping re-review findings by the reviewer's own type taxonomy shows the loop is dominated by one type: duplicate_owner accounts for 265 of the 490 typed findings. More telling is the conditional: across all types, a finding follows a same-type finding that was already acted in 39.6 % of cases (duplicate_owner) down to 4.8 % (thin_wrapper), and follows a review where the type was merely present in 83.4 % / 14.3 %. So the dominant review pattern is not “one instance at a time” in the sense of a chain that shrinks per round; the reviewer keeps reporting the same class of finding while the plan is being edited, which is what an un-converged resample looks like. The instance indices in the store (e.g. duplicate_owner-1, -2, …) reset per review, so a same-type finding two reviews apart is a new instance of the same class, not a re-report of the same id.

§5 — Counterfactual replay of five damping mechanisms

The replay walks each plan's timeline of content states — one per committed revision of the plan file — and asks how many reviews each mechanism would have run over the same three weeks of history, and which acted findings stop gating as a result. Read the two columns together: every mechanism that damps also removes gating. M1 (one review per build dispatch, the lower bound for an author hold-then-review flag) would have run 1051 reviews and lost 246 acted findings to the coarser sampling; M4 with longer settle windows runs 858 (1800 s) and 622 (3600 s) against the actual 300, losing 237 and 238. M2 (re-review gates only on findings whose unit changed) and the round caps cut hardest: M2 runs 76 reviews, 3.9× fewer than history, M3 with K=1 runs 57, but each de-gates 159 and 180 acted findings respectively — the reduction comes from the reviewer no longer being asked about most of what it found.

M5 is the only mechanism that removes a sixth of the reviews for two per cent of the gating: dropping followups and authored comments from the fingerprint removes 50 reviews (300 → 250) and de-gates just 49 acted findings, against 159 for M2's 3.9× reduction. Its cost is concentrated where the plan's comments were the trigger — exactly the 45 document-only pairs of §2. The conclusion for the review gate is that document-unit churn is the cheapest thing to damp, and that the round cap and settle-window knobs buy their reductions by sampling content states less often, not by reviewing the same content less.

Caveats

Four limits bound what these numbers can carry. (1) The store is live and was growing during the measurement; every figure is a snapshot at 2026-10-06T04:18Z and the script must be re-run to refresh it. (2) “Acted” is a unit-level association, not a causal claim: a finding lands in a unit whose digest changed, which is necessary but not sufficient for the change to have produced the finding. (3) 187 of 492 findings name a code path that appears in more than one section and are attributed to the first candidate; the unclassifiable share is reported rather than hidden. (4) The five mechanisms are text-only counterfactual replays over committed plan states, not implementations: they model what content each state would have been reviewed at, and count the acted findings that stop gating, but they cannot replay which findings a reviewer would have raised at a different sampling. The M4 numbers additionally assume each committed content state marks the end of a quiet period.
