§1 — The question and the answer so far

The lead asked whether requiring an evidence document for every section implemented is overkill, and whether it would be better to ask each section for a visual record where one applies and to write one concise, sourced closure overview when a sprint or significant plan closes.

It is practical, and most of the machinery exists. Four findings support it.

  • Every landing is written down four times (§2). Only two of the four are needed to rebuild the other two.
  • The per-node fragment costs a worker a median 7% of its run, and evidence accounts for one commit in six in this repository (§3).
  • Three quarters of fragments are never opened by an agent after they are written (§4).
  • A closure overview built without reading any fragment kept 47 of 60 sampled fragment claims, with every sentence sourced. The 13 it lost were test timings, pass counts, hashes and identifiers (§7).

The cost in worker time is smaller than the lead's sense of churn suggests. The churn is real, and it sits mostly in commits, in the coordinator's splice step and in the repository's size, not in the worker's run.

§2 — What one landing writes today

A section that lands leaves the same result in four places. The first two are written by the worker, the third by the coordinator and the fourth by the promotion.

RecordWhereWritten byHolds
Fragment docs/evidence/fragments/<plan>/<node>.html worker prose account of what landed, with tables and sometimes a figure
Landing line manifest landing:, copied to the plan as a c-close comment worker, then coordinator one sentence: what landed and the measured result
Landed record anchor docs/evidence/archive/<plan>-landed.html coordinator, by append_evidence the fragment, spliced into the plan's cumulative record
Run record docs/state/reckon/runs/<run-id>.json promotion commits, gate command and verdict, tests added, changed lines, negative control, suite delta, review scores, worker time

The landing line and the run record together carry what happened, who checked it and how. The fragment and the landed record anchor restate them in prose. The worker prompt makes the fragment mandatory for every implementation node, and the build skill makes the splice one of three operations in every landing.

§4 — Who reads it

  • Fragments: 489 of 1,923 were read, 729 read calls in all. Coordinators made 483 of those calls, which is consistent with a coordinator opening a fragment to verify and splice it, though the census did not separate that from later reference.
  • Landed records: 72 of 213 were read, 530 calls, almost all by coordinators. Only 17 landed records are cited by a plan other than their own.
  • Research documents: 8 of 49 were read by an agent. This undercounts their use: they are written for the lead, and 38 of the 49 are cited by at least one plan.

This census counts agent reads only. It says nothing about which documents the lead reads in the browser, which is the reader the lead's question is about.

§5 — This was decided once already

Plan landed-record-at-closure, shipped 2026-10-04, ruled that a node makes one write, its plan comment, beside the run record, and that the landed document is synthesised once at closure. It added the closure guard that refuses a terminal status without an evidence record, and reckon evidence synthesize, which builds a landed record from plan comments and run records.

Fragments arrived on 2026-09-28 to stop workers colliding on one landed file. Plan worker-authors-its-own-record, done 2026-10-13, then made the fragment a requirement on every node. That restored the per-node document the earlier plan had removed. Fragments have been added at 79 to 499 a week since.

§6 — Recommendation

  1. Drop the mandatory per-node fragment. A node leaves its landing line, its run record and its gate log. This is the one deletion: it removes the fragment rule from the worker prompt and the build, edit and create skills, and the splice from the landing beat.
  2. Ask for a figure where the section has something to show. A node whose result is a measurement, a comparison or a rendered surface writes one figure under docs/figures/<plan>/ and names it in its manifest. A node with nothing to show writes none; there is no quota.
  3. Write one sourced overview at closure. When a plan or sprint closes, build one page from the plan's done-whens, landing lines, run records, stored reviews and figures. Every claim carries a commit, a run id, a gate command with its exit status, or a figure path. The existing synthesiser and closure guard are the starting point.
  4. Leave research documents as they are. 40 commits in 30 days is no churn, and they are the class the lead reports using.
  5. Keep a place for what a run record cannot hold. A negative result or an explanation of a mechanism belongs in the landing line when it fits in a sentence, and in a research document when it does not.

The prototype in §7 bears on item 5: nothing it lost was a finding, so the landing line and the gate log are enough for routine nodes.

§7 — What a closure overview loses

A prototype script built a closure overview for three shipped plans and one closed sprint from the plan's sections and done-whens, its section comments, its run records, its stored reviews and its figures. It was barred from reading fragments or the existing landed record. Twenty factual claims were then sampled from each plan's fragments (seed 20261114) and counted as lost when one of their numbers, commits or test names appeared in none of the permitted sources.

PlanFragmentsLanded recordOverviewClaims lost of 20Unsourced sentences
a-live-worker-can-be-reached516 KB, 1,659 words12 KB, 1,568 words90 of 19
a-review-is-sized-to-its-risk3826 KB, 3,083 words54 KB, 3,672 words20 of 33
a-window-paces-the-lane19255 KB, 28,665 words50 KB, 4,287 words20 of 67
  • What was lost is detail. The 13 lost claims are suite durations (68 passed in 37.12s), pass counts of a full-suite arm, two content hashes, a session id, a message file name and two fixture values. For 12 of the 13 the overview still states the claim and lacks only those tokens. The worker classed five of them as negative results because they sit in a negative-control sentence; the missing token in each is a duration or a count, and the control's verdict survives in the run record.
  • Little needs authoring. The script ran in 3.4 seconds for all four pages. Each section needed one authored headline sentence, 24 across the three plans; everything else was mechanical.
  • An overview is not always shorter. For the 38-fragment plan it is twice the size of the landed record, because its routine table has 114 rows, one per run. A closure overview should fold retries into one row per landed node.
  • A section with no landing line has no outcome. One of the 24 sections, moved in from a followup, had neither a closing comment nor a run row, and the overview could only say so. Without fragments, the landing line is the only prose account, so the closure guard should refuse a section that lacks one.
  • Figures carry across. The plan overviews embed 6 and 21 existing figures, and the sprint page 26.

The sample is three plans, and the loss test is mechanical: it matches tokens, so it cannot tell whether an explanation of a mechanism survived in substance. No lost claim was classed as a mechanism explanation, which is weak evidence that such prose is rare in fragments, not proof.

§8 — Limits of these measurements

  • Rounds caused by record defects. Not recoverable from run records (§3).
  • Coverage. 987 of the 1,141 implementation runs in the window have no stream on disk; retention moved them to an archive store that keeps no stream. The stream measures rest on the other 154.
  • Census review. An independent review re-ran the census on a compute partition. The share-of-run and turn figures reproduced exactly. The read counts came out under 1% higher (733 fragment reads over 493 files against 729 over 489) because the coordinator transcripts are live files that grew between the two passes.
  • Prototype review. An independent review reproduced the three size pairs byte for byte and the zero count of unsourced sentences. Its re-run counted 12 lost claims where the prototype counted 13; it traced the difference to two pass counts matching by accident inside commit hashes in its own re-run, so 13 stands. The comparison cannot be re-run as delivered: the prototype kept its cache of run records in node-local /tmp, which is gone.
