§1 — Corpus, method, and shelf life

Read on 2026-09-04 between 07:00Z and 08:20Z across four committed crew ledgers — imas-ambix, reckon, imas-codex and nova — totalling 2,567 run records, together with the raw stream.jsonl of 1,931 of those runs and two live readings of the metered backend's own account surface.

Every figure here has a shelf life. The ledgers are written by live coordinators and the corpus grew from 2,555 to 2,567 during the session itself; the meter moved from 12.0% to 18.0% in eighty minutes, none of it from this work. Treat each number as a reading with a timestamp, not a constant, and re-derive rather than quote. That is the same rule that made the routing reference's hand-written priors wrong, which is why this document states method alongside every result.

Two method choices matter for reproducing anything below. Runs classified infrastructure-failure, malformed-node, correct-refusal, pre-existing-failure or negative-result, and those flagged scope_changed, are excluded from outcome statistics: they measure the transport or the estimate rather than the worker. And a resumed session records its whole accumulated context on every turn, so run-level input sums are an upper bound on true consumption; where that mattered, outliers above 60M input were dropped and the fact is stated.

§3 — Cost is a fixed floor per node, not a function of work done

Regressing input tokens on delivered lines over 1,738 usable runs of the default model:

input_tokens = 8,732,597 + 51 × changed_lines

The marginal cost of a delivered line is 51 tokens; the intercept is 158% of the median run's entire input. Cost is therefore a property of how many nodes run.

Larger nodes are five times more efficient per line delivered, and splitting fixed work into k nodes multiplies its cost by k — 2.00×, 3.99× and 7.97× computed for two, four and eight, flat across every work size tested. A node-size cap would therefore raise burn while appearing to control it.

§4 — Four instruments that read as informative and measure nothing

This is the section with the longest reach beyond the cost question. In each case a number exists, is consulted, and cannot discriminate.

Gate pass rate is saturated. Stratified by declared specification level and declared difficulty over usable runs, the two effort tiers are indistinguishable at 0.939 (n=115) and 0.930 (n=172), and no configuration with a real sample falls below 0.93. The cause is structural rather than statistical: the gate is a check the implementing worker wrote and then ran against its own diff. Rework rate — a node whose declared write paths a later run in the same plan re-touches — discriminates eightfold on the same corpus and reverses the cost ordering, and the orchestrator's own redispatch rate agrees in sign (0.115 against 0.265 on exact-level nodes).

Reached independently, by a method with nothing in common with this one. A coordinator session reviewing brief conformance on one day's merged commits in this repository reports that 11 of 40 merged code commits met their brief in letter only, and that the day's gate pass rate was not reproducible — 71.6% counted by row against a claimed 92%. Those figures are that session's measurement and are recorded here as reported rather than re-derived; what matters is that reading forty commits against their briefs and reading 2,582 run records against their gates reach the same conclusion, and that neither was looking for the other's answer. A gate written and run by the same worker can saturate at 0.93–1.00 while a third of the work it passed satisfies its brief only literally: the two observations are not in tension, they are the same defect seen from opposite ends.

The competence horizon inherits that saturation and can never fire. It is derived as the largest declared size whose success rate clears 0.8. Because every size bin clears it, the horizon collapses to the largest node anyone has ever declared: 48.0 worker-hours for the default configuration's 1,029-run slice, against a maximum declared estimate of 10.0 hours anywhere in the corpus. So the one fence that nominally bounds node size is unreachable by construction, and its CompetenceLimit refusal has almost certainly never been raised. It is not a loose fence; it is a fence calibrated on a broken instrument.

Model-active time is largely unlogged, so worker time means wall clock. The generation/machine split exists in code and its own docstring states the hazard — a node running its own suite has a wall rate far below its generation rate. It reaches the ledger rarely:

Where it is measured, median model-active time is 240.9 s against 371.6 s elapsed and 60.6 s of machine time. For the other 77%, batch waits and test runs are counted as worker time indistinguishably from generation. The consequence for fencing is that the binding time_budget is wall-clock, so a node waiting on a submitted job can exhaust its fence having spent nothing, while another burns the full floor well inside it.

Coordinator spend is unrepresentable. A search of every key across all 2,567 records finds no field naming a coordinator, orchestrator or author, because coordinator sessions are not crew runs. A second session reproduced this against a live run pointer rather than committed records — its agent, budget, competence and execution_fit blocks all present, no coordinator attribution anywhere — which locates the gap in the run representation itself rather than in what promotion keeps. Consequence: any change moving reasoning from worker to coordinator scores as a saving, because only the half that got cheaper is metered. Relatedly, suite_delta is populated on 0 of 2,567 runs and gate_check.log_digest on 32 of 876 — two fields designed for independent verification that carry nothing.

§6 — Candidate lanes can be screened for free

A published benchmark reports pass@1, dollar cost, output tokens and steps per model and effort. Only the steps column bears on a metered lane, and it transfers: the benchmark records 37 steps for the default configuration on goal-only work, and our own open-specification runs at the same configuration measure 39 — agreement to 5%. Multiplying a candidate's published step count by the per-step figure of §3 predicts its burn before anything is dispatched.

The dollar column is actively misleading here. The cheapest family in dollars is the most expensive in steps, needing 49 to 102 steps to reach the incumbent's accuracy — 1.3 to 2.7× the incumbent's burn, so it cannot win even at zero rework. It is disqualified without a single dispatch. The next candidate undercuts the incumbent's step count only at its two lowest tiers, both scoring 15 to 34 points lower, so it must hold rework at or below 0.24 to break even: worth one qualification slice, not a pilot on faith. The one model better on both axes is refused on this account outright — a probe returns HTTP 400, "not supported when using Codex with a ChatGPT account", while the incumbent answers the same probe — and being API-key billed it would move spend to a card rather than reduce metered burn.

Qualification is days rather than weeks, because rework is not the lagging indicator it appears to be: across 493 observed instances its median lag is 1.1 hours, with 81.7% surfaced within 6 hours and 96.1% within 24. A shadow slice contributes none of it, though — a shadow is never merged, so nothing can later re-touch its paths. Ten shadow pairs cost 2.0–2.7% of a weekly window and establish steps, input and controlled agreement with the incumbent's own named check; rework needs a live gated pilot, and discriminating 0.07 from a 0.24 break-even needs roughly 20 runs for two standard errors and about 40 to be comfortable.
