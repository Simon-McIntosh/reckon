§1 — What was measured, and why this is not a survey

A consuming project reorganised its whole portfolio and then reviewed it: 67 plans across 17 sprints over roughly five weeks, producing 35 validated findings and a ten-row plan priority order. The assessment below is that portfolio's practice reported against reckon's model by the session that did the reorganising — practice as lived rather than as documented. It is worth more than a design review because every misfit below is a workaround somebody already paid for, and because the reporter had no stake in reckon's schema being right.

Two claims were checked against reckon's source rather than accepted: the absence of a sprint-item remove operation, and the treatment of section anchors in dependency refs. Both hold, and the second turned out sharper than reported.

§2 — What held, including one position worth defending harder

Composed-live item state is right, and it earned its keep. The schema refuses a persisted status or impl on a sprint item and composes both from plan HTML at read time. That refusal is what let the review detect drift at all: had item state been stored, the sprint would have agreed with itself while disagreeing with reality, and nothing would have surfaced. The reporting session called the position right after having been refused by it, which is the useful direction for that verdict to arrive from.

Per-plan priority with derived sprint order is the correct altitude. This was the design decision most at risk of being wrong — a portfolio that thinks in sprints first would have made the whole ranking schema the wrong shape. It sequenced plans and sections, consumed the persisted ranking directly as dispatch order, and the derived sprint order matched what it would have written by hand.

Decisions are the highest-value state in the system — the locks and their rationales, above sprints, blockers and followups. Worth knowing when deciding what the surface should show first.

§3 — The misfits, ranked by cost

GapWhat it costs todayAssessment
Section-level dependencies are parsed and ignored A plan whose early rungs are dispatchable and whose last rung waits reports effective_status=blocked, hiding roughly eight hours of ready work. Measured twice in one portfolio. The most expensive gap, and see §4 — it is worse than absent.
Write-scope disjointness has no schema home Four sprints run in parallel partitioned by which subsystem they mutate, because concurrent sessions must not collide in a shared checkout. That is a real membership force expressed nowhere. Reckon already enforces path exclusivity at dispatch time and models nothing at planning time. The constraint exists in both places; only one has a vocabulary.
Must-not-run-concurrently is invisible to the graph Suite exclusivity and shared-checkout serialisation live in memory files and agent instructions. A planner cannot see them. Same root as the row above: scheduling constraints are not dependencies and have no edge type.
Cross-project obligations are invisible to both roadmaps Three obligations lived as followups pointing at foreign plans that had shipped — excluded from the owning project's pending work because the target was terminal, and from the consumer's because it was foreign. Coped with by hand-appending followups in the other repository. The cheap first move is an audit check: a followup naming a foreign plan that is terminal. The full fix is a handoff that surfaces in both projects.
Closure gated by a foreign result A plan is execution-complete but must not ship until a gate on another plan runs. Not depends_on, because nothing blocks execution — the closure is what is gated. No edge type. Coped with by a comment, which is invisible to the DAG.
A decision gated by a foreign plan's outcome The dominant blocker class in that portfolio is open decisions, and decision_blockers models the plan-local case only. A decision waiting on another plan's result has no representation.
No sprint-item remove operation Repairing one duplicated membership required a wholesale set of a hand-reproduced twelve-kilobyte item list, because move refuses a duplicate. Verified: the only sprint-item verb is move. A data-loss shaped hazard for a one-row repair. Cheap to fix.
Item done_when rots silently One item's done_when contradicted its own plan's locked deferral decision for six days. It is prose duplicated from the plan with no freshness check. Either compose it from the plan's gate state or stop storing it. A second copy with no equality check is the same defect class as a persisted item status.
Derived active sprint is arbitrary under parallel actives With four sprints legitimately active, the derived pointer picks one, so "focus" and "the active sprint" have quietly diverged. Multiple-active as a warning is correct for that portfolio. The derivation needs to admit a set, or the concept needs renaming.
Legacy effort letters poison capacity silently A four-hour letter placeholder made a sixteen-hour programme look trivial at sprint level; the per-plan uncalibrated warnings did not carry. A sprint-level "capacity N% uncalibrated" rollup would surface it where the decision is made.
Priority ranks read as a sequence when they are lanes Ranks one to three were concurrent lanes. A consumer treating rank as strict order would serialise work that should fan out. Rank ties, or an explicit concurrency marker. The reasons vocabulary hints at it but does not say it.
The reasons vocabulary lacks a cost reason A two-hour item ranked almost purely on cheapness-to-clear was labelled roi for want of a better word. Same discovery shape as the missing isolation category: a closed vocabulary found short by use, not by design.

§4 — The sharpest finding: a feature parsed and then discarded

Section-level dependencies are not missing. The ref grammar is documented as [project:]slug[#stage], and parse_plan_ref captures the stage group — so an author can legitimately write plan-depends-on="catalog#s3" and reasonably believe they have expressed a dependency on one rung. The roadmap then contains no reference to that stage at all, so blocking is decided whole-plan.

That is worse than the feature being absent. An absent feature produces a malformed-ref refusal at the write boundary and the author learns immediately. A parsed-then-ignored feature accepts the input, silently overstates blocking, and reports the plan as blocked while dispatchable work sits inside it. The author cannot tell the difference between "reckon does not support this" and "reckon supports this and the work really is blocked", and the roadmap — the thing every coordinator reads to decide what to run — is confidently wrong.

plan-verifies already consumes slug#section anchors, so the machinery to resolve one exists in the codebase. The gap is that dependency resolution never asks.

§5 — Three surfaces nobody used, and the honest reading

Across 67 plans: zero milestones, empty north stars, a timeline with six id-only events nobody reads back, and three active sprints whose start and end dates were empty until backfilled during the review. It would be comfortable to read that as an adoption problem. The more useful reading is that these surfaces ask for information the work does not naturally produce, while the surfaces that did get used — sprint membership and ordering, blockers, followups, and above all decisions — ask for information somebody was going to write down anyway.

Graph handles are the one genuine discoverability failure rather than a shape failure. A campaign spanning two repositories is exactly what a derived cross-project closure is for, and the reviewing session met the feature only once, in a tool docstring — no plan, no skill walkthrough, and no audit finding points at it. A feature nobody adopts because it is obscure needs a different remedy from one nobody needs, and these two have been indistinguishable in the usage data until now.

§6 — What this implies for the work in flight

The persistence and composition layer is validated: refusing derivable state, composing it live, and ranking per plan all survived contact with a portfolio that did not design them. The gaps cluster in one place, and it is not persistence — it is expressiveness of the edges. Six of the twelve rows above are a relationship the graph cannot express: partial dependency, mutual exclusion, closure gating, foreign decision gating, cross-project obligation, and concurrency lanes. Each was coped with by a comment or a memory file, which is to say each is currently invisible to exactly the tool built to answer what can run now.

That is a coherent next body of work rather than twelve scattered repairs, and it should be sequenced by cost: the audit check for a followup naming a terminal foreign plan and the sprint-item remove operation are hours; section-anchored blocking is the one that makes the roadmap stop being wrong; the exclusion and closure-gating edges are a design question that deserves its own decision before anyone writes a schema for them.
