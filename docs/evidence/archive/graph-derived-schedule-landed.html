Schedule from the graph, not from dates — landed evidence

Five sections, all landed, every landing commit proven an ancestor rather than read off its own summary. The plan sat at full implementation for eleven days while two of its five gates could no longer be run at all — that is the part of this record worth keeping.

Record scope

The Graph tab blanked for every endpoint without a handle, a sprint could not open its own graph, and the flow strip was empty for any sprint with no dispatched runs because it drew a fixed 48-hour window rather than a derived schedule. Two gates that had stopped being able to run

A scope review found this plan at full implementation with every deliverable present and two of its five stated measures unable to produce a green result. Neither failure was in the feature. Both were repaired by the surface-contract plan, which owns assertions that describe what the code used to do, so one repair was made once rather than three times across the plans that tripped over it.

GateWhy it could not runRepair
§3 — graph surface geometry Asserted a stylesheet rule by exact dict equality. A later, unrelated viewport-containment fix added min-height: 0 to that rule, so a test about the header failed because of a change to something else. Assert the property the test is about rather than a snapshot of the whole object.
§5 — rendered derived flow Rendered against the live wall clock with fixture stamps fixed at 2026-09-02–04. The surface's own 60-hour retention filter dropped every fixture bar, packing one lane where two were expected. Red since roughly 2026-09-06 and failing on every later date. Inject a fixed clock, as the three sibling schedule tests already did. The expected lane count stays at two.

The second repair had a wrong answer available that would have looked right: change the expectation to one lane. That passes today only because the fixtures have aged out of the window, and it fails on fixture-era dates — replacing a date fuse with a differently dated one. The clock injection is what makes the assertion true on every date.

Measured over the six migrated files at the surface-health plan's authored base and again at its head: 9 failed, 39 passed, 5 skipped, 4 errors becomes 52 passed, 5 skipped. Nine fewer failures, four fewer errors, none added.

What the eleven days cost, and what would have caught it

Nothing reported this. The plan read complete, sat on the sprint's critical path, and its two dead gates were invisible to every surface that decides what runs next. The plan carries no gate element, so its measures existed only as prose and could not block or be reported unmet — and no plan in this sprint carried one, so strict gate enforcement was configured and firing on nothing.

The instrument that found it was a section-level review that quoted each stated measure and then tried to run it, rather than reading the implementation percentage. That is the difference between a number that is true as a recorded value and a number that says whether the work holds.
