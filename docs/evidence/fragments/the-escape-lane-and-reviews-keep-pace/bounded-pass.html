A pass ends when its launch budget is spent, and every launch says what it cost

Why a pass needs an end. An admission pass launches until its queue is empty, and while it runs it holds every seat it has freed, so a request that becomes admissible half a second after the pass started waits for the whole pass to finish. The verdict log says how long that is. Across the 93 pass records the live admit service wrote between 2026-11-14T20:02:00Z and 2026-11-15T03:09:48Z — a window of 25,668 s, all on the clive lane with the gate open, over several code stamps because the service reloads between passes — the launch stage accounts for 10,810 s, 42% of the window, and one pass spent 1,413.7 s in it. Every one of those records predates this node's commit, so every one describes the unbounded pass.

What the budget bounds, precisely

admit_once reads the deadline once at the head of its launch loop and stops starting launches when it has passed, so a pass spends at most its budget beginning launches and leaves the rest of the queue untouched — no attempt, no hold, no verdict row — for the next pass. A launch already in flight still runs to completion, so a pass ends within one launch of its budget rather than at the deadline; with the budget read at the loop head this is the population most exposed now, because a single launch is tens of seconds and the longest passes begin many of them.

The budget comes from flight: admission.pass_budget_seconds, defaulting to 60 when the key is absent, read through flight.admission_pass_budget_seconds, which falls back to the default for anything that is not a finite positive number. The pass result carries launch_budget_seconds and launch_budget_exhausted into the pass verdict record, and each launched row carries launch_seconds, slowest_step and slowest_step_seconds from per-step accumulation over handover, dispatch and stamp. Nothing was added to the schema, so the new keys ride the existing verdict rows.

The instrument the cases rest on

Three things had to be true of the cases themselves before the behaviour above was evidence. The budget they hand the pass is 30 s against a stubbed 20 s launch, which fits two launches where the flight default of 60 s fits three, so a pass that ignored its argument and fell through to the default launches one more than the case expects. They resolve their lane through _config_with_lane, which deep-copies the fixture's config before writing to it, rather than through a local resolver that wrote the lane path into the module-level config and left it there for every later test in the process. And one case drives the pass with no argument at all against a flight config published by the test, which is the only end-to-end exercise of the resolution path, while another asserts the shared fixture config is unchanged after a pass.

The live profile of the service

The 120 s py-spy record --idle of the live admit service after landing is not in this fragment. This node cannot produce it, for two reasons that are both properties of the deployment rather than of the work. The change is not landed — a worker commits to its worktree and the coordinator lands and restarts — so a profile taken now would sample the unbounded pass and say nothing about this change. And the live service is not on the host this node runs on: the fleet allocation is job 1313886 on 98dci4-clu-2001, no process with admit in its argv runs on that node, and the verdict file is nevertheless written continuously (last pass ended 2026-11-15T03:25:49Z, file modified 20 s before it was read), so the service belongs to another node and possibly another session's process.

What stands in for it is the 93-pass measurement above: the pass verdict records are the service's own account of where each pass spent its time, at the stage granularity this node extends to individual launches. The profile the done-when asks for should be recorded after the restart, by whoever lands the change, against the pid owning the restarting service — py-spy record --idle is not runnable against a process this session cannot see.
