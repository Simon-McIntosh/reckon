Node-size bound — prior lock

This frozen record preserves the superseded choice on node-size-bound for the dispatch-cost-per-durable-node plan, so a later reader can see what was decided, on what evidence, and why it was reopened within the same session.

§1 — What was locked

node-size-bound = 1h, locked 2026-09-04 by Simon McIntosh. Dispatch was to refuse a node whose declared estimate exceeded one hour, naming the bound and the split.

The rationale recorded at the time: median input per node rose 8.9× from a one-hour to a four-hour declared estimate, with the cliff between one and two hours — 1,232,983 tokens at ≤1 h against 5,858,234 at ≤2 h, a factor of 4.8. A two-hour bound would therefore concede most of the available saving. At one hour the sustainable ceiling was calculated at roughly 1,792 durable nodes per week against 287 at then-current node sizes, covering an observed 1,204-run peak at a burn of about 0.67.

§2 — What refuted it

The comparison never normalised for delivered work, and the bands do not deliver the same amount: the one-hour band delivers a median 133 changed lines and the four-hour band 868. Regressing input tokens on delivered lines over 1,738 usable runs gives

input_tokens = 8,732,597 + 51 × changed_lines

so the marginal cost of a delivered line is 51 tokens and every node pays a fixed floor first. Splitting fixed work into k nodes multiplies its cost by k — 2.00×, 3.99× and 7.97× measured for two, four and eight — and the larger bands are five times more efficient per line, 6,221 against 51,319 input tokens per line. An independent check agrees from the other direction: 564 nodes per week at the floor is 4.93 B input, a burn of 1.89, against 1.97 measured from recorded tokens over the same period.

A separate measurement removed the quality argument for a length fence. Binned by measured tool steps rather than declared hours, rework is flat at 0.37 from the 0–19 band through 80–99 and the gate holds between 0.87 and 0.91, so a ninety-step run comes back no more often than a ten-step one. The earlier signal that larger nodes rework more was keyed on declared hours, which track how messy a task is rather than how long its run went.

§3 — What replaced it

The key was reopened and relocked to warn-only: the fence reports an oversized node without refusing it, because the measured cost argument for refusing ran backwards while the judgement that very long autonomous runs carry unmeasured risk is not contradicted by any signal available here — rework and gate outcome are the only two, and both are coarse. Warning costs nothing and preserves the hook should a sharper quality signal later show what neither captures.

The cost levers named in its place are those that are not transfers between budgets: reducing what every tool step re-reads, consolidating work so fewer nodes each pay the floor, and explicit lane routing. See §4 and §9 of the live plan.
