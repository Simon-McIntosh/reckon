§1 — A coordinator asked for a dispatch order and got the wrong shape, twice

Reckon derives two different answers to what should run next and presents one of them. Asked about a sprint it returns pending work, a ready set, a critical path and a schedule window, and it answers well. Asked about an outcome — a capability that spans several sprints — it derives the closure, reports it in an endpoints array, and says nothing about whether that closure can be shipped at all.

Measured on 2026-09-14, on this repository, by a coordinator that had the evidence in front of it. Asked for the dispatch order of a capability spanning four sprints, it read handle: null on both derived endpoints in the payload it had already fetched, wrote that the capability “is a graph, not a sprint”, and then produced a list of per-plan invocations anyway. Authoring one handle afterwards turned eleven plans into a single addressable target reporting six of eleven shipped, depth seven and average width 1.571 — none of which any sprint view can show. The information was all derived already. Only the actionable gap was unreported.

The sprint shape is not wrong; it is the only shape that presents itself. That is a property of the surface rather than of the reader, and it is why this plan exists separately from the one that discovered it.

§2 — Thirty findings about graph health, none about an unshippable outcome

roadmap emits thirty wiring finding codes. They cover dangling, invalid, inactive, non-executable, contradictory and cyclic dependencies; sprint-order inversion; duplicate and missing sprint membership; unoriented plans; undeclared north stars; stale reviews. Not one concerns a graph handle.

The only handle errors are raised on the way in: a caller who already names a handle that does not resolve is told so. A plan that is structurally an endpoint — a sink with live members beneath it — and carries no handle is silent, and its closure is unaddressable by any invocation. Discovering that requires reading the endpoints array and noticing a null, which is exactly what did not happen above.

Compare the treatment of orientation. A live actionable plan declaring no north star raises unoriented-plan, so the gap announces itself. An unshippable outcome has no equivalent, and the two defects have the same character: a relationship the schema supports, that nothing requires, and whose absence no surface reports.

Done when a plan that is structurally an endpoint and carries no graph handle raises a wiring finding naming it and the members it would reach; a plan that carries one raises nothing; the finding is severity-graded so that it is visible to a reader without refusing execution, since an unshippable outcome is a reporting gap rather than a malformed graph; and the finding is absent for a sink with no live members beneath it, which is an ordinary leaf rather than an outcome.

§3 — One call, both answers

A coordinator deciding what to start needs the sprint answer and the outcome answer together, because they optimise different things and disagree in useful ways. A sprint concentrates work on one theme and therefore on one set of files; a closure follows dependency edges and picks up disjoint work naturally. The same ready node can be first in its sprint and irrelevant to the outcome the lead actually wants, or the reverse.

Today those are two calls with different targets, and the second requires knowing the handle exists. What a reader deciding dispatch order needs is: for each ready item, which outcome it serves, and whether that outcome is shippable as one target.

Done when a single roadmap read reports, for every item in the ready set, the endpoint closures that item is a member of and whether each is addressable as a ship target; an item belonging to no closure says so explicitly rather than by omission; the sprint-shaped fields keep their present meanings and their existing tests pass unchanged; and the addition is derived rather than stored, so no closure membership is written to any resource.

§4 — A completion figure that cannot reach its own total

A closure member closed as will-not-do stays in the denominator and never ships, so shipped_of_total caps below total permanently and the closure can never report complete. Measured on 2026-09-14 by causing it: closing the local-worker plan as abandoned left lane-aware-dispatch at six of eleven with four members still to land, making ten of eleven the best reachable figure, forever.

Removing the dependency took the member out of the closure entirely and the total fell to ten, which repaired that instance and does not repair the class: it worked only because the dependency turned out to be unnecessary. A closure whose abandoned member is genuinely depended upon has no such escape.

Two candidate repairs, and the choice is not obvious. Either an abandoned or superseded member leaves the completion denominator the way a deferred one does, on the reasoning that a plan which will never ship is not outstanding work; or a stage-scoped dependency resolves membership to the section rather than the plan, which is the more precise reading of what such an edge means and would also stop a closure inheriting sections nobody depends on. The first is smaller and fixes only this symptom. The second changes what a closure is.

Done when a closure containing a member in a terminal non-shipped state can reach a completion figure that reports the outcome as complete when every member that can ship has shipped; a closure whose members are all live is unaffected, proven by its figure being unchanged; and the reported total states which members it counts, so a reader can tell a closure that is finished from one that is capped.

§5 — Why this is a plan and not a section on the one that found it

Both findings were discovered while closing concurrency-and-schedule-visibility, whose subject is which nodes may run together and what should run next. The roadmap-shape finding is adjacent to that subject; the completion-denominator finding is about closure accounting and is not. Neither was in that plan’s declared scope, and landing them there would have grown it past what it was authored for — the pattern its own residual section already records as the way two deliverables were nearly lost.

It also would have blocked a sprint. That plan is the last open item in S15, its declared scope is complete, and holding it open on two findings that are not its subject would report a sprint as unfinished for work it never owned. Recording where discovered work goes is the point of the rule, not paperwork around it.

The dependency on that plan is real rather than decorative: §4 here needs the closure machinery that plan’s residual section describes, and both findings are documented in its comments with the measurements that produced them.

§ Followups

Report the unshippable outcome before answering both shapes

Take §2 first. It is the smallest of the three, it needs no decision, and it is what would have prevented the measured failure: a coordinator read handle: null in a payload it had already fetched and still produced a sprint-shaped answer, because nothing reported the gap as actionable. A finding makes it announce itself the way unoriented-plan already does for orientation.

§3 follows §2 rather than preceding it, because a combined read that reports closures a reader cannot ship is worse than the two calls we have now — it would surface an outcome and then fail to act on it. Settle handle-finding-severity before §2 is dispatched; it changes whether the finding blocks execution, and the precedent it is measured against is one that reports sixteen times and gates nothing.

§4 is independent of both and should not be dispatched until denominator-repair is locked, because the two candidates differ in what a closure is rather than in how the same thing is written. Its evidence lives on concurrency-and-schedule-visibility §8 with the measurement that produced it.
