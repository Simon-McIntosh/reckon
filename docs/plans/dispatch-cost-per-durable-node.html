§1 — What the meter actually charges

The metered lane carrying the default backend was running at 7.6× its sustainable rate when this plan was written: 13.0% of a 168-hour window consumed with 1.7% of the window elapsed, measured from the backend's own account surface at 2026-09-04T07:17:34Z. The account had already been exhausted once, on 2026-09-03, with a reset eight days out.

Three measurements taken together explain the burn, and they rule out the two remedies a reader would reach for first.

The full measurement record — corpus, method, exclusion rules, and the findings this plan does not act on — is What A Dispatched Node Costs, And What Our Instruments Cannot See. Read it rather than this plan when the question is how a figure was obtained: it carries the fence inventory, the burn history by window, the node-size trend behind the 2026-09-03 exhaustion, and the reason the competence horizon can never fire. The sections below carry only what drives a deliverable.

The meter charges total context read, including cached tokens. Two disjoint intervals bracket the charging formula. From window start to 07:04:04Z the meter moved 12 points against 376 M total input tokens and roughly 15 M fresh ones; from 07:04:04Z to 07:17:34Z it moved one point against 21.5 M total and 0.52 M fresh. The total-input hypothesis is consistent across both intervals at a weekly capacity of 2.1–3.1 B tokens; the fresh-input hypothesis disagrees with itself by a factor of 2.4 and is refused. So the prompt cache cannot buy headroom: measured hit rate is already 96.0% median across 1,970 runs, with only 5.1% of runs below 0.90, and 23.0 B of the 23.6 B input tokens consumed were cached re-reads that the meter charged anyway. Caching buys latency and money. It does not buy quota, and no amount of further cache work will.

Burn is steps multiplied by context, and effort buys steps. Median turn count per run is 1, so cost accrues over tool steps inside a single agentic turn. At the same declared specification level the high-effort configuration takes 23–32% more tool steps than medium and consumes 21–34% more input, and input tracks steps almost linearly at roughly 203 k tokens per step. That per-step figure is a property of our fences, plan text and worktrees rather than of the model, which is what makes it transferable in §7.

§2 — The routing instrument is saturated

Gate pass rate cannot rank a worker configuration, because every configuration passes. Stratified by declared specification level and declared difficulty, restricted to usable runs, the two effort tiers are indistinguishable: high passes 0.939 over 115 runs against medium's 0.930 over 172. Across the whole ledger no configuration with a meaningful sample falls below 0.93. An instrument whose readings span 0.93 to 1.00 has no discriminating power, and the reason is structural rather than statistical: the gate is a check the implementing worker wrote and then ran against its own diff.

An unpaid-for signal already sits in the ledger. A node whose declared write paths are re-touched by a later run in the same plan did not stay done. That rework rate discriminates by a factor of eight where pass rate discriminated by nothing, and it reverses the cost ordering once charged:

High effort wins at every specification level, so the pinned effort: high in the host routing layer is already correct and effort is not the lever this plan pulls. Two independent proxies agree on the direction — path rework, and the orchestrator's own redispatch decisions — which is why the conclusion is drawn from them rather than from the saturated gate.

Rework carries one honest confound: a node in a heavily churned file accumulates re-touches for reasons unrelated to its quality. The redispatch column is the check on that, being an explicit judgement about the same node rather than a coincidence of paths. Where the two disagree in magnitude they agree in sign, and §3 records both rather than blending them.

Two fields designed for exactly this measurement are unpopulated: suite_delta on 0 of 2,563 runs, and gate_check.log_digest on 32 of 876. Populating suite_delta makes the gate measure the suite against the node's base revision rather than the worker's own new test, which is an independent check obtained without a second dispatch.

§3 — A derived routing surface, and no numbers in prose

Deliverable: extend reckon capabilities with rework, tool steps, input tokens and cost per durable node, and expose crew(project, view="routing") returning, per (model, effort, spec level, role) across every mounted ledger: sample count, pass rate, rework rate, redispatch rate, median tool steps, median input, and cost per durable node with the sample count that earned it.

This is derived state and therefore Python, per the binding rule that the surface renders and does not derive: a CLI caller, an MCP read and the SPA must all get the same figure. Done-when is a numeric assertion against a synthesised ledger fixture, plus a parity check that the served surface and the tool return identical rows.

The reference document keeps the procedure and loses the numbers. skills/reckon-ship/references/effort-routing.md currently carries hand-written pass-rate priors that this plan's measurements contradict, which is the documented failure mode of a recorded absolute that moves on its own. It keeps the specification-ownership table and the decision procedure, and cites the call in place of every figure. An orchestrator then reads current routing from a live surface rather than from a table whose last edit it cannot see.

The surface must carry the back-loading, or the next reader reverts the change. Raising effort raises per-run cost 26–52% immediately and returns it as avoided rework at a 1.1-hour median lag, so for the first few hours a coordinator watching a meter sees a change that is failing. Recording that in a research document is not sufficient: the person who reverts it will be reading the meter, not the document. So the routing surface reports the rework-charged figure beside the per-run figure, and labels which of the two a short window can yet reflect. This requirement came from a coordinator watching a live wave through the change rather than from the measurement.

Why §9 precedes this section, stated here because this is where it will be ignored. A routing surface shipped before coordinator accounting exists gets used to justify moving reasoning from worker to coordinator, and scores every such transfer as a saving, because only the metered half got cheaper. The ordering is therefore not a preference about which section is more interesting: shipping this one first produces confident wrong decisions for as long as the gap remains, and they are the expensive kind because they carry a number.

§4 — Node count is the lever, because cost is nearly all fixed

An earlier reading of this section held that node size was the lever and that splitting a large node saved 2.2×. Normalising cost by delivered work refutes that, and reverses it. The earlier figure compared a four-hour node against four one-hour nodes without checking that the two delivered the same amount, and they do not: the one-hour band delivers a median 133 changed lines and the four-hour band 868.

Regressing input tokens on delivered lines over 1,738 usable runs, with resumed-session outliers dropped:

input_tokens = 8,732,597 + 51 × changed_lines

The marginal cost of a delivered line is 51 tokens. Every node instead pays a fixed floor of roughly 8.7 M input tokens before it changes anything — the orientation a worker cannot skip, re-read at every tool step: the plan section, the fences, the code it must not break, the gate. Cost is therefore a property of how many nodes run, almost independent of how much each delivers.

Larger nodes are five times more efficient per line delivered, and splitting fixed work into k nodes multiplies its cost by k — 2.00×, 3.99× and 7.97× for two, four and eight, computed from the fit above and flat across every work size. Smaller nodes do carry lower rework, 0.24 against roughly 0.49, but that advantage is nowhere near the fivefold fixed-cost penalty it would have to overcome.

The independent check closes on the same number from the other direction: 564 nodes per week at the 8.7 M floor is 4.93 B input, a burn of 1.89, against the 1.97 measured from recorded tokens over the same period. The fleet's whole consumption is its node count times the floor, so the sustainable ceiling is about 298 nodes per week at today's floor, whatever size they are.

So a node-size cap is refused, and the two real levers are named instead. The first is consolidation: prefer one node that carries a coherent piece of work to several that each re-pay the floor, which makes the existing per-configuration competence horizon the right shape of fence and a size cap the wrong one. The second is the floor itself, which nothing has ever measured or targeted: it is 158% of the median run's whole input, so halving what a worker must read before it can act doubles fleet capacity. That is a larger prize than any model substitution in §7 and it is the one this plan had missed.

Deliverable: report the floor rather than fence the size. Dispatch records each node's orientation cost — input consumed before its first write — and the routing surface of §3 carries the floor per (model, role, spec level) so a rise in it is visible. Done-when: the floor is derived from ledger data with its regression coefficients and sample count, and one recorded run's orientation cost is asserted against its stream.

§5 — An undeclared specification level is not a well-formed node

965 of 1,586 high-effort runs on the default backend carry no declared specification level. They fell through to the backend default, so the routing map could not see them and the calibration slices they belong in do not exist. Declaration has since reached 98.3% over the last eight days by convention alone, which is exactly the state that regresses silently.

Deliverable: --spec-level becomes a required property of the pre-dispatch contract, refused with exit 2 naming the missing property, on the same footing as the seven properties already checked. The purpose is calibration visibility, not a cheaper effort tier — §2 settled that the cheaper tier costs more. Done-when: a dispatch omitting the flag exits 2 naming spec-level, and the ship skill's checklist carries it as an eighth box.

§6 — A burn fence, from figures the fence already holds

The existing budget fence holds a wave when utilisation crosses a ceiling, which is a fence on position. It cannot see rate, so a lane burning a week's quota in a day reads clear until the moment it reads spent. That is what happened on 2026-09-03.

BudgetState already carries rate_limit_period_minutes and resets_at, so utilisation divided by window-elapsed needs no new probe and spends nothing. Deliverable: preflight and crew(view="budget") report the burn multiple beside the utilisation, and the pre-flight summary names it. At the reading above the fence would have shown 7.6× on the first day of the window rather than a clear lane at 13%.

Reporting only, not holding, until a week of measured burn exists: a rate fence calibrated on one reading and wrong in the holding direction stops every worker on the machine while the provider is serving, which is the asymmetry the shelf-life setting in the host layer already exists to avoid.

§7 — Candidate lanes, screened before they are run

A published benchmark reports pass@1, dollar cost, output tokens and steps per model and effort. Only the steps column bears on our constraint, and it turns out to be predictive: the benchmark records 37 steps for the default backend at high effort on goal-only work, and our own open-specification runs at the same configuration measure 39 — agreement to 5%. Steps transfer, so the benchmark screens candidate lanes against our meter for free, and multiplying a candidate's step count by the 203 k per-step figure of §1 predicts its burn before anything is dispatched.

The dollar column is actively misleading on a subscription lane, and the figure below is what that misleading looks like. Each family traces a curve as effort rises; better is up and to the left.

The screen therefore disposes of the two subscription-eligible candidates before either is dispatched. The luna family is disqualified outright: at 49 to 102 steps it burns 1.3 to 2.7× the incumbent, so even at zero rework it cannot beat the incumbent's cost per durable node. Its dollar cost of one to sixty-one cents per task is irrelevant to a metered lane and is precisely the reading that would have selected it. The terra family is marginal: only its high tier at 34 steps and its medium tier at 25 undercut the incumbent's step count, and both score 15 to 34 points lower on the benchmark, so terra at high effort must hold rework at or below 0.24 to break even. That is a narrow enough margin to be worth one qualification slice and not worth a pilot on faith.

The frontier model is the only genuine improvement on both axes — 73% at 26 steps against the incumbent's 69% at 37 — and it is refused on this account: a probe returns HTTP 400, "not supported when using Codex with a ChatGPT account", while the incumbent identifier answers on the same probe. It is API-key billed, so it would not reduce account burn at all; it would move spend onto a card. It is therefore defined and not routed: a backend in the host layer with a spend ceiling, reachable only by an explicit override or a shadow run. The configuration schema already carries environment and auth_check, so this needs no schema work.

Qualification is days, not weeks, and the reason is measurable. Rework was the obvious candidate for a slow lagging indicator, and it is not: across 493 observed instances its median lag is 1.1 hours, 81.7% has surfaced within 6 hours and 96.1% within 24. So a live gated pilot yields a trustworthy rework figure inside two days. A shadow slice cannot contribute one at all, because a shadow is never merged and nothing can later re-touch its paths; what a shadow measures immediately is steps, input, and controlled agreement with the incumbent's own named check on the same node at the same base revision.

Sample size sets the confidence. Discriminating a 0.07 rework rate from the 0.24 break-even needs roughly 20 runs for a two-standard-error separation and about 40 to be comfortable, so the pilot band is 20 to 40 rather than the ladder's current ten. The ladder's existing bar — pass at or above 0.8 over ten runs — must be replaced by cost per durable node before any candidate is qualified, because §2 showed that bar is met by every configuration we have ever run and therefore selects nothing. That ordering is the reason §3 precedes §7 in execution.

§8 — What this plan does not do

It does not change the pinned effort, which §2 shows is already right. It does not add a finer effort dial: the two existing tiers differ by 23–32% in steps and are indistinguishable in quality, so a finer dial would add configuration surface with no measured axis to tune, while the axis that moves cost nine-fold is node size and is already continuous.

It does not split test authorship from implementation by default. That pattern answers the real defect of §2 — a self-graded gate — but it doubles dispatches and context on a lane that is already over capacity, and the test author needs the same interface specification as the implementer, so it only applies at exact and guided levels, which is where rework is already lowest at 0.07 and 0.15. Populating suite_delta obtains an independent check for one field rather than one extra dispatch per node. Reserve the split for nodes whose risk is declared elevated or critical.

§9 — The ledger sees one half of the system

The floor of §4 decomposes cleanly, and it is linear rather than quadratic: input per tool step is flat at 170–230 k from the shortest runs to the longest, so cost is tool steps × roughly 175 k and a step is the unit worth reducing.

Run length does not degrade quality in this data. Binned by measured tool steps, rework is flat at 0.37 from the 0–19 band through 80–99, and the gate holds between 0.87 and 0.91. A ninety-step run comes back no more often than a ten-step one. The earlier reading that larger nodes rework more was keyed on declared hours, which tracks how messy a task is rather than how long its run went. Both signals are coarse, so this bounds the claim rather than settling it: a length fence would buy no measured quality, which is a reason to warn rather than refuse.

The lever this plan reached for next is partly an accounting illusion, and the ledger cannot tell. Nodes declared exact run 26 tool steps against guided's 37, which reads as a 1.9 M saving per node. Two measurements undercut it. First, the specification text a coordinator actually writes does not grow with the declared level: median done_when is 585 characters at exact against 729 at guided, so the visible specification is no larger and the step difference cannot be attributed to specification completeness — it may be that simpler tasks attract the exact label, the same selection effect that made pass rate useless in §2. Second, and structurally, no run record carries any coordinator field at all: a search of every key across 2,567 records finds nothing naming a coordinator, orchestrator or author, because coordinator sessions are not crew runs. So the coordinator's spend in producing a tighter specification is not merely unmeasured, it is unrepresentable.

That asymmetry biases every routing decision in one direction. A change that moves reasoning from worker to coordinator always looks like a saving, because the ledger only meters the half that got cheaper — and the coordinator is spending a budget of its own, on a lane with its own ceiling. The routing reference already states the principle, that an exact specification written for a high-effort worker is the same reasoning paid for twice; what is missing is the instrument that would let anyone check it.

Independently reproduced, and on the other half of the store. A second session checked a live run pointer rather than the committed records this section was derived from, and found its agent, budget, competence and execution_fit blocks all present with no coordinator attribution anywhere in the record. The gap is therefore in the run representation itself rather than in what promotion chooses to keep, which is what makes it a schema change rather than a promotion-path fix.

This plan's own cost is not the spend question, and saying so matters because the figures invite the wrong reading. Sixteen worker-hours sounds large against a lane measured at 7.8× sustainable, but hours are not the currency: §4 established that cost is node count times the floor. At six to twelve nodes this plan is 2.0–4.0% of a weekly window, or 2.5–4.9% of the headroom remaining at the reading above. Deferring it saves a few percent and postpones the only instrument that would make the other ninety-six legible — so the aggregate dispatch rate is the spend decision, and this plan is not it. Reasoning about it in worker-hours is precisely the error the section is about.

Two consequences for this plan. The levers that survive are the ones that are not transfers: reducing what every step re-reads, where the 120 k of accumulated context and the 16.7 k of agent documents are both ours and both multiply by every step; consolidating work so fewer nodes each pay the floor, tempered by the drift caution above; and moving work to another lane, which is at least an explicit transfer rather than a hidden one. And the routing surface of §3 must carry a coordinator column, or it will keep recommending transfers and scoring them as savings.

Deliverable: record coordinator spend against the nodes it dispatches, so a specification-tightening change can be judged on total cost. Done-when: a dispatched node's record carries the coordinator session's identity and its token spend for the authoring turn, and the §3 surface reports worker-plus-coordinator cost per durable node alongside the worker-only figure it reports today, with one test asserting the two differ on a node whose coordinator spend is non-zero.

§ Decisions

What node-estimate bound should dispatch refuse above (§4)?

Relocked to warn-only after reopening. The measured cost argument for refusing ran backwards: cost is 8,732,597 + 51 x changed_lines, so splitting fixed work into k nodes multiplies it by k (2.00x, 3.99x, 7.97x measured) and the larger bands are five times more efficient per line, 6,221 against 51,319. A refusal at one hour would therefore have raised burn while appearing to control it. The quality argument for a length fence is also unsupported here: binned by measured tool steps, rework is flat at 0.37 from 0-19 through 80-99 and the gate holds 0.87-0.91, so a ninety-step run comes back no more often than a ten-step one; the earlier signal was keyed on declared hours, which track task messiness rather than run length. But rework and gate outcome are the only quality signals available and both are coarse, and the lead's judgement that these workers drift on long autonomous runs is not contradicted by any instrument we have. So the fence reports an oversized node without refusing it: warning costs nothing, keeps the hook if a sharper signal later shows what neither captures, and avoids encoding a refusal whose own evidence points the other way. Prior lock frozen at docs/plans/archive/dispatch-cost-per-durable-node-node-size-bound-locked.html.

How should the API-key-only frontier model be treated?

Defined in the host layer with a spend ceiling and reachable only by explicit override or shadow run. It is the only candidate better on both axes, but it is billed per token rather than metered, so routing to it converts a quota problem into a spend problem. Defining it costs nothing until used and provides a lane that survives window exhaustion.

What should dispatch do when no specification level is declared?

Refuse as not-dispatchable, naming the missing property. A silent default reproduces the failure mode where 965 runs became invisible to calibration, and silence is the failure that costs most here because it looks like success.

Should the locally served lane take exact-level work before qualification?

It measures 0.95 gate pass over 19 exact-level runs on free hardware, which is attractive but rests on the saturated instrument of §2 and on one specification level. It goes through the same ladder as every other candidate, on the repaired metric.

Where do current routing figures live?

A derived Python surface read through the crew tool, with the reference document keeping only the ownership table and the decision procedure. Gating dispatch on the slice was rejected for now because it would refuse on thin samples; regenerating the document was rejected because a written figure is a snapshot with a fuse and only helps readers of that one file.

Should the burn-rate fence hold a wave or report only?

Report until a week of measured burn exists. The asymmetry favours reporting: a rate fence wrong in the holding direction stops every worker on the machine while the provider is serving, and a fence that is merely late announces itself when the position fence fires.

Reopen the node-size bound — normalising by delivered work reverses it

Locked choice: node-size-bound = 1h, on the reasoning that median input rises 8.9x from a 1-hour to a 4-hour node with the cliff between 1h and 2h.

What changed: that comparison never normalised for delivered work, and the bands do not deliver the same amount — the 1-hour band a median 133 changed lines, the 4-hour band 868. Proposal: reopen the key and lock warn-only, or retire it, and replace the fence with the two levers the fit names — consolidation, for which the existing per-configuration competence horizon is already the right shape, and reducing the orientation floor, which is 158% of a median run's whole input and which nothing has ever measured.
