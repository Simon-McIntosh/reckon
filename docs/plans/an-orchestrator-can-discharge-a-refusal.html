§1 — A refusal with no way through is a stop wearing a guard's clothes

Reckon's promotion surface is careful, and it should be. It refuses a passing gate with no evidence, a verifier that edited source, a commitless promotion that did not say so, a run whose session is still resumable. Each of those refusals was added because something went wrong without one, and none of them is the problem here.

The problem is what an orchestrator does next. A refusal is only a guard if there is a way through it: a waiver to state, a different verdict the evidence does support, or a named owner for the repair. Where there is none, the guard stops being a check on the work and becomes a wall across it — and the run behind that wall does not disappear. It sits as a live pointer, counts against the project's unreconciled total, and eventually refuses every new dispatch in the project through the grace fence. One unanswerable refusal propagates into a fleet-wide stop.

Measured on this project, 2026-09-18. The run r-20260915T093545453615-the-guard-holds-at-the-merged-head has been terminal for three days. It is a merged-head verification and it succeeded: 505 passed against 20 failed at first parent 08a0bfbe, 518 against the same 20 at merge 9b92f6fe, failure-set difference 0 added and 0 removed. Its worktree is clean, its head is an ancestor of the branch, and an independent review scored it 20 / 20 / 19 / 19 / 19. Everything a promotion is supposed to check is satisfied, and it cannot be promoted.

§2 — The refusal surface grew and the discharge surface did not

That row is not unlucky. It is the expected outcome of an asymmetry that can be counted, and the counting is the point — this is a ratio, not an anecdote.

So 33 of 42 refusals tell the caller that something is wrong and not what to do about it. That is the mechanism by which a careful surface becomes an impassable one: every incident adds a refusal, because a refusal is what prevents a recurrence, and nothing adds the matching discharge, because at the moment a guard is written the case it wrongly catches has not happened yet.

The count also explains why this is worth a plan rather than a fix. Repairing the one guard that caught this row leaves the ratio at 33 of 42, and the next orchestrator meets the next one.

§3 — Three exits, and all of them are closed

An orchestrator holding a refused promotion has exactly three commands and a fourth move that is not a command. Every one of them fails on this row, for a different reason, which is what makes it a good specimen rather than a one-off.

Every move available to an orchestrator holding this run, and the reason each is closed. The fourth is not a command, which is the finding.

Promote. Refused, and correctly by its own rule: the verdict asserted is passed and the cited log's recorded exit is non-zero. See §5 for why the log nonetheless shows a pass.

Waive. There is no waiver for this refusal. The five that exist were each written for a specific earlier incident, and this is not one of them. An orchestrator cannot state a reason, because there is no field to state it in.

Discard. reckon crew discard removes a non-running pointer without recording promoted evidence. It would clear the count, and it would delete the only record that this verification happened — the merged-head measurement, the failure-set difference, the review scores. Discard is the move a blocked orchestrator is most likely to reach for, and it is the one that destroys evidence. A surface whose only escape from a wrong refusal is to erase the work is training its users to erase work.

Repair the guard. Not a command, unbounded in size, and on this occasion blocked outright: reckon/crew/promotion.py is held by a live run in another coordinator's session. The orchestrator's last resort depends on a file it may not touch.

§4 — Every refusal names its discharge

The rule this plan proposes is one sentence: a refusal that cannot be discharged is not permitted to exist. Each of the 42 carries one of exactly three things, and which one is a property of the refusal rather than a judgement made at the call site:

  • A waiver — the check is a heuristic, so a caller who can see more than the check can state a reason and proceed. The reason lands on the ledger row, so a waived promotion is permanently distinguishable from a clean one.
  • An alternative verdict — the evidence does not support what was asserted but does support something else, and the refusal says what. A gate asserted passed whose log shows otherwise can be re-promoted as failed or not-run; the refusal should name that rather than leave the caller to infer it.
  • A named owner — the refusal is correct and nothing the caller can say changes it, so the message names what must change and who currently holds it. This is the case that has no surface at all today, and §6 is about it.

Why a discharge must be recorded rather than silent. The objection to waivers is real and this plan takes it seriously: a guard everyone can wave through is a guard that reports a protection it does not provide, which is the failure this project has recorded repeatedly. The answer is that a discharge is evidence, not permission. It carries the refusal's identity, the caller's stated reason, and a timestamp, onto a committed row. That makes waivers countable — and a refusal being waived often is the signal that it is mis-specified, which is exactly the information nobody has today, because the current alternative is a discard that records nothing at all.

Done when every refusal reachable from reckon crew complete carries one of the three, asserted by a test that enumerates the refusal sites and fails on any that carries none — so the property holds for refusals added later, which is the whole point. Range the assertion over the refusal surface itself rather than over a list of known refusals: a fixed list is a snapshot of a moving number and goes stale the first time someone adds a guard. The negative half is asserted too: a refusal whose discharge is a waiver still refuses when the waiver is absent.

§5 — The guard that produced this reads a proxy as the thing itself

The specific defect is worth its own section, because the obvious repair is the wrong one and the right one generalises.

At reckon/crew/promotion.py:464:

if recorded != 0 and re.search(r"\bcommand not found\b", log_text):

It refuses a passed verdict when the cited log's recorded exit is non-zero and its text contains command not found anywhere. Both conjuncts matter, and missing the first is easy — this coordinator quoted the guard without it and was corrected by a peer before cutting a node for the wrong repair.

What it catches here. Line 77 of the row's log is "bash: line 1: rekon crew frobnicate: command not found\n". That is a doctor test's own fixture, misspelled rekon deliberately, sitting inside captured test output. The recorded exit is 1 because the gate is judged by delta against a red base — the project's own rule, since a shared checkout carries peer failures in flight. So both conjuncts hold on a run that passed, and the guard cannot tell the difference.

Why "distinguish fixture text from shell output" is not the repair. It is the first thing that suggests itself and it is insufficient, because the exit status genuinely agrees with the string. The deeper fault is that the check answers did the gate command run? with a negative signature — it infers absence from the appearance of a diagnostic. A negative signature is a proxy, and this project has recorded the same shape before: a commit sha checked by width rather than resolved, a stream path read as the backend, an unchanged pid read as no re-exec.

The positive control is already in the log. A pytest run that emits its own summary line — N passed, M failed in Xs — is direct evidence that the command ran, and direct evidence outranks any inference from a substring. Done when a log carrying the gate's own completion evidence promotes despite the diagnostic appearing in captured output, and — the half that must be written first — a log carrying the diagnostic and no completion evidence still refuses. Without the second assertion a guard that never fires passes the test.

The specimen is the row itself: its log is at ~/.config/reckon/crew/runs/r-20260915T093545453615-the-guard-holds-at-the-merged-head/after.log, and a successful promotion of that row is the end-to-end proof. Note that reckon crew complete is the sole promotion route and its gate-evidence requirement is unconditional there while the underlying ledger call stays permissive for internal callers, so a test written against the ledger call passes without ever exercising the refusal.

Provenance, checked rather than assumed. 941fc6d4 added the guard and no repair commit exists anywhere in history. An open followup on a-live-worker-can-be-reached has recorded this defect as reproduced, owned elsewhere and has a repair in flight since 2026-09-15, naming no run. Two coordinators independently found no such run. That record is itself an instance of the plan-staleness problem: a sentence true when written, false since, and read as current by everyone downstream — and in this case it kept a row blocked for three days on an assurance nobody was acting on.

§6 — When the repair is in a file someone else holds

The third discharge in §4 — a named owner — has no surface today, and what filled the gap on this occasion is worth writing down precisely because every step of it was a compensation.

To find out who held promotion.py, this coordinator read crew(project, view="scopes") for the claim map, matched the claiming run to its session, then identified the owning coordinator by walking process ancestry from that session's follower consumer pid up through uv, bash and claude to the zellij server — because a crew session id is not a harness session name and nothing maps between them. Only then could a message be sent.

That worked, and it is not a procedure anyone should have to rediscover. reckon crew directory already names every live coordinator, its repository, its plans and whether it is still dispatching. The missing step is the join: a refusal whose repair lands in a claimed path should name the claiming run, its node and its coordinator session, so the orchestrator's next move is to contact a named peer rather than to conduct an investigation. scope-conflict at dispatch already does exactly this — it refuses and names the owning run — so the surface exists and is simply not reached from the promotion path.

Done when a refusal whose stated repair path is a repository file reports that file's live claim if one exists, naming the run, the node and the session, and says so explicitly when the file is unclaimed — because "no claim" is the answer that lets an orchestrator proceed and is currently indistinguishable from not having looked.

§6b — A discharge that erases what it discharged

The sections above ask whether a refusal names a way through. This one asks what the way through records, and the answer is currently: nothing that distinguishes an authorised act from the act the guard exists to catch.

Reported from imas-efit, 2026-09-21, with its reproduction. A run is dispatched with --write-path A --write-path B. It refuses on a scope boundary, correctly. The coordinator extends its scope through crew resume --advice, naming C and D. The worker writes C and D. At crew complete the boundary guard refuses:

run '...' changed paths outside its declared write scope:
tests/CMakeLists.txt, tests/data-manifest.json.
Retry with --accept-path PATH REASON for each deliberate companion path

The paths were deliberate and authorised. The run's recorded claim simply never learned about it: write_paths is captured once at dispatch (reckon/crew/node.py:469 and :492) and nothing on the resume path updates it, so an advice-granted extension and a genuine boundary violation are the same fact at promotion time. The reporting run was r-20260918T080928315298-curate-input-redundant-transform-removal, which refused three times, correctly each time.

The ergonomics are the smaller half. --accept-path is the same escape hatch for both cases, so a coordinator who extended scope legitimately and one waving through a genuine sprawl produce identical ledger rows. Any later reader — a fitness audit that scores scope sprawl as a defect class, a calibration pass, a human asking what happened — sees one shape where there were two events. That is the same failure this plan names elsewhere: a stable record whose referent has moved, and a true statement standing in for the one that was needed.

Two shapes, and they are not equivalent. Either the resume path records the granted extension on the run so the guard reads the current claim rather than the dispatch-time one; or --accept-path gains a second form that says an authorised extension is being recorded rather than a violation waived. The first is cleaner because it removes the discrepancy instead of annotating it, and it also makes the extension visible to the scope registry while the run is still live — which is where a concurrent peer needs it. The second is cheaper. Whichever lands, the ledger must keep which of the two it was, because that is the fact the current row destroys and the only one that makes the repair worth doing.

Write paths. reckon/crew/node.py, reckon/crew/resumption.py, reckon/crew/promotion.py, and the test file covering the scope boundary.

Done when a run whose scope was extended through crew resume --advice promotes without --accept-path, asserted; a run that wrote a path nobody granted is still refused, asserted, because a repair that opens the guard is worse than the defect; and the promoted rows of those two runs differ in a named field, asserted by reading both rows back — the distinction the current record cannot express.

§7 — What this plan does not own

Making the return half of the orchestrator loop automatic — reviews that dispatch themselves, an inbox that prompts, signals that are true — belongs to an-orchestrator-is-bound-by-its-inbox. That plan is about duties nothing reminds you to discharge. This one is about a duty you are trying to discharge and cannot, which is why they are siblings rather than one plan: the first is defeated by forgetting, the second by refusing.

The unfenced plan write belongs to plan-writes-are-not-fenced, and the rendering of the counters to monitor-renders-what-it-is-given. Neither is touched here.

This plan also does not propose relaxing any existing guard. Every one of the 42 refusals stays. What changes is that each acquires a stated way through, and the one in §5 acquires a correct test for the thing it is actually asking about.

§ Decisions

Does each refusal get its own named waiver flag, or is there one keyed waiver that takes a refusal identity and a reason?

A flag per refusal is how the current five arose, and it is safe in the sense that each is deliberate, reviewed and narrow. It is also how the surface fell 33 behind, because a flag is only written once a refusal has wrongly caught something, and by then someone is already stuck. One keyed waiver — --waive <refusal-id> "<reason>" recorded on the row — makes the discharge surface grow with the refusal surface by construction and makes waivers countable per refusal. Its cost is that every guard becomes waivable at once, including guards whose whole value is that they cannot be argued with.

What becomes of a row whose refusal is correct and whose repair is owned by someone else?

Today it stays unreconciled, and once past the grace window it refuses every new dispatch in the project — so one row another session must fix stops a fleet. The alternatives are a recorded disposition that stops the clock without promoting (honest, but a new way for work to be quietly parked), or leaving the pressure in place on the argument that a blocked fleet is what gets the repair done. The measured case argues against the last: this row applied that pressure for three days and produced no repair, because the coordinator who could feel the pressure was not the one who could act on it.

/reckon-ship an-orchestrator-can-discharge-a-refusal §5

A lane change strands the ordinary resume, and the only discharge is another lane change

A measured refusal with no discharge except an action that consumes a finite resource, reported by a peer session on nova and recorded here because it is this plan's subject rather than that session's. After crew resume --backend <other> --reason … moves a run to a codex-family lane, the new session captures no session id in any authority. The next ordinary crew resume --run <id> --advice is then refused:

no session id in the live pointer, the run's own stream or the promoted ledger row; all three were consulted

and a same-backend move is refused with already uses backend. So the only way to continue the run is another lane change to a different backend name — measured twice on one run, r-20260921T091427817554, moving clive → codex → codex-luna → codex, each hop the only available continuation.

The cost is not data loss and that is what makes it easy to miss. The worktree and the ledger row survive every move, so nothing is destroyed and no alarm fires. What is consumed is the run's continuity and a lane name: each continuation starts a fresh session needing a full handover brief, and the set of distinct backend names is finite, so a run can be resumed only as many times as there are lanes left to move it to. A refusal that discharges by spending a bounded resource is a stop with a countdown on it.

The refusal message is also honest about the wrong thing. It reports that three authorities were consulted, which is true and reads as thoroughness — but the defect is that none of them was ever written, and the message cannot distinguish an absence from a failure to record. Naming the consultation instead of the capture points the reader at the read path when the fault is on the write path.

The remedy is one write at the moment that always happens: a codex-family session records its session id into the live pointer on its first turn, exactly as the other lanes do, after which the ordinary resume works and no lane name is spent. Assert it by performing a lane change and then a plain resume, since a test that stubs the capture asserts the behaviour this finding says is missing.

Reported by a peer as no action needed tonight; recorded because a cross-session message is not a record, and both parties' context boundaries would have taken it.

/reckon-ship an-orchestrator-can-discharge-a-refusal

A fourth refusal shape, and it is the one this plan's three-way taxonomy does not cover: a refusal that names its discharge correctly and gives a reason that is false for the instance. Reported by a peer coordinator on a three-week-old worktree, verified here at reckon/crew/routing.py:534-542.

Worktree reclamation withholds an unintegrated tree with this reason: its HEAD is not reachable from the integration revision, so removing it would destroy the only copy of that commit; merge it or discard it deliberately. By §4's test that is a well-formed refusal — it names two discharges, and a caller who reads it knows what to do.

The premise is the problem. Unreachable does not imply sole copy. The peer's worktree held ten PNGs byte-identical to files already on main, arriving there by another route, and a capture index a week older than main's. The work was superseded, not lost. So the tree is clean forever and unreachable forever: it satisfies one half of the reclamation condition permanently and the other never, and accumulates by construction. Three weeks, no live pointer, and nothing reporting it.

Why this matters to the taxonomy rather than being one more bug. §4 asks whether a refusal carries a waiver, an alternative verdict, or a named owner. This one carries a named action and still misleads, because the sentence a coordinator reads asserts a consequence — removing it would destroy the only copy — that is checkable and, here, wrong. A refusal's reason is itself a claim, and an unverified claim in a refusal is more dangerous than a bare refusal: it converts a discharge the coordinator could safely take into one it believes is destructive. The peer did not remove the tree for three weeks, correctly by the text and unnecessarily by the facts.

So the discharge contract needs a fourth clause: a refusal that asserts a consequence must be able to demonstrate it. Here the demonstration is one command — whether the tree's content exists anywhere reachable, which is a different question from whether its commit does. Cheap, and it converts a permanent withholding into either a safe removal or a refusal whose reason survives inspection.

This is the positive-control rule again, arriving in a fourth place. The refusal infers sole copy from unreachable commit, which is a negative signature; the positive control is asking whether the content is present on the integration branch. Recorded here rather than on the peer's figures plan because what generalises is the refusal contract, not the figures.

So the unifying rule stated across these plans is wrong as written, and the correction is sharper. It is not prefer positive evidence to a negative signature. It is move the discriminator out of the free text and into a field the process declares. Polarity is incidental; provenance is the whole thing.

And the out-of-band signal this guard needs is already recorded. The run carries its exit status separately, and the guard already reads it — recorded != 0 is the first conjunct. What it lacks is any record of what the verdict means. A gate judged by delta against a red base legitimately exits non-zero, so exit status cannot decide it; the run needs to declare its base failure count and its added count, which promotion already accepts elsewhere as --waive-suite-delta and failure-attribution machinery. A guard reading two declared integers needs no substring search in either direction.

That reading also explains why the one repair that landed today worked. The wait-marker change did not swap a negative test for a positive one; it moved from does the field exist to what do the structured fields say, validated — probe and terminal parsed as lists, a trivially-true probe rejected, a prose none honoured. Declaration validated structurally, not inference over text. Same move, and it is the only one of the four that is in the tree.
