§1 — A feature that is parsed and then discarded

The ref grammar is documented as [project:]slug[#stage] and parse_plan_ref captures the stage group. reckon/roadmap.py contains no reference to that stage at all. So an author may write plan-depends-on="catalog#s3", reasonably believe they have declared a dependency on one rung, and receive whole-plan blocking instead. Nothing refuses the ref and nothing reports the discard.

This is worse than an absent feature, and the asymmetry is the whole argument. An absent feature is refused at the write boundary by validate_for_write and the author learns in the same second. A parsed-then-ignored feature accepts the input, silently overstates blocking, and then reports the plan as blocked while dispatchable work sits inside it. The author cannot distinguish reckon does not support this from reckon supports this and the work really is blocked, and the roadmap — the one artifact every coordinator reads to decide what to run — is confidently wrong.

Measured in a consuming portfolio of 67 plans: a catalog plan whose first two rungs were dispatchable and whose third waited on a demonstration reported effective_status=blocked, hiding roughly eight hours of ready work. The same shape recurred inside a second sprint, where three profile-owned sections were ready while one waited on a separatrix section. Both were coped with by prose that the graph cannot read.

The resolution machinery already exists elsewhere in the codebase: plan-verifies consumes slug#section anchors today. The gap is that dependency resolution never asks.

§2 — Honour the anchor, or refuse it ✓ landed 2026-09-15

Landed. A dependency ref carrying a section anchor blocks only the sections that need it: a plan declaring depends_on="other#s3" reports its unanchored sections dispatchable (ready_sections) while the anchored one reports blocked (blocked_sections), and the containing plan stays in the roadmap ready set rather than reporting blocked. A staged ref naming a section absent from the target is a missing-dependency-section wiring finding, not a silent satisfaction or a whole-plan block. A bare ref keeps byte-identical whole-plan blocking. Mechanism landed in 9e666e3 (merged 1790e61); a verification node re-checked every clause of the done-when at the merged head, asserted the eight-hour portfolio case as a fixture showing the work surfacing, and ran the roadmap tests 39 passed — 38 pre-existing unchanged, plus the fixture. Full record: §2 landed.

There are exactly two honest outcomes for a staged dependency ref, and the current behaviour is neither. Either blocking honours the anchor — a dependency on catalog#s3 blocks only the sections that need s3, and the plan stays dispatchable for everything else — or the write boundary refuses a staged ref with the grammar stated, so an author who wanted partial blocking learns immediately that it does not exist. Accepting the ref and discarding its meaning is the one option that misleads, and it is what ships today.

Honour it. Refusal would be defensible if partial dependency were a fringe request, but it is the dominant real shape: plans here are authored as ordered rungs, and a late rung waiting on foreign work is the normal case rather than the exception. Whole-plan blocking then systematically understates what can run, which is the failure the roadmap exists to prevent.

Derived state stays derived. A staged dependency does not introduce a new persisted field: effective_status continues to be computed, and gains the ability to be partial — a plan with a satisfied section-level prerequisite for its ready rungs is not blocked, while the specific sections whose anchors are unmet are reported as such. Readiness answers a question about sections, and the plan-level answer becomes a rollup of them rather than a separate judgement.

§4 — Execution is finished and closure is not the same question

A plan can be execution-complete while its closure waits on something else: the work is done, the tests are green, and it must not be marked shipped until a gate on another plan runs. depends_on is the wrong edge, because nothing blocks execution — asserting it would idle work that is ready. Today this is carried in a comment, which is to say it is invisible to the tool that decides what may close.

The same shape appears one level down for decisions: a decision gated not by a question someone must answer but by another plan's outcome. The existing decision-blocker machinery models the plan-local case well and has no representation for a foreign result.

Both are the same missing distinction — an edge that gates a transition rather than an activity. Design them together, and settle whether they are one edge type with a stated transition or two, before writing a schema for either.

§5 — What this plan deliberately does not own

Mutual exclusion — write-scope disjointness, suite exclusivity and shared-checkout serialisation — is not in scope here, and duplicating it would create exactly the competing authority this repository keeps paying for. That work belongs to concurrency-and-schedule-visibility, which already models comparing file claims and deriving lanes and sits near completion. When both land, an orchestrator can ask which sections are ready and which of them may run together; neither plan answers both alone, and neither should.

Priority lane semantics — that ranks one to three are concurrent lanes rather than a sequence — belongs to the review priority schema rather than to the dependency graph, and is recorded as a finding against that resource instead of a section here.

§ Decisions

A dependency ref carries a section anchor. What should happen?

The grammar accepts it, the parser captures it, and the roadmap discards it. Whole-plan blocking measurably hid eight hours of dispatchable work in a real portfolio.

Partial dependency is the dominant real shape here, not a fringe request: plans are authored as ordered rungs and a late rung waiting on foreign work is normal. Refusal is honest but throws away the case that matters; leaving it is the only option that actively misleads, since the author believes they expressed something the system silently drops.

Is a closure gate the same edge type as a decision gated by a foreign outcome?

Both gate a transition rather than an activity — one gates a plan reaching a terminal status, one gates a decision being lockable. Modelling them as one edge with a stated transition is smaller; modelling them separately is clearer to read in a roadmap payload.

One edge carrying the transition it gates, because two edge types would be two representations of one concept with nothing keeping them in step. The plan states the trade honestly - one edge is smaller, two are clearer to read in a roadmap payload - and the tie is broken by a rule this repository already holds twice over. The readability advantage of two types is a presentation concern, and presentation is derived here: a payload that carries the gated transition as a field can render a closure gate and a foreign-outcome decision gate as distinct rows without either being a distinct stored edge. Choosing two stored types to buy that would put the distinction in the schema where every reader must keep the two definitions aligned by hand, which is the same defect as writing one fact into two records with no mechanism keeping them equal - the argument that settled where per-step execution evidence lives on a sibling plan this sprint. The concrete consequence for the work behind this lock: section 4 implements a single edge whose transition field names what it gates, plan-terminal or decision-lockable, and the roadmap derives the two presentations from that field rather than from two parsers. If a third transition is ever gated, it is a new value rather than a third schema.
