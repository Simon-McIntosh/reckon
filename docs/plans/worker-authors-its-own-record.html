§1 — A rule stated in two places that disagree

The composed worker prompt requires a worker to author its own landing record: “Append your landing record to your own section of the plan and your evidence anchor to the cumulative evidence record; both live in this worktree and both go into your final commit.” It then forbids the same act four lines later — “Do not edit reckon plan or index state. Return outcome data to the orchestrator.” — and in between explains how to edit the plan without touching the version and modified meta lines.

The fence agrees with the requirement rather than the prohibition: dispatch adds the plan HTML and the cumulative evidence path to an implement node's exclusive write paths automatically. Measured on one dispatch, three declared write paths became five in the composed prompt, the extra two being exactly those.

Five workers in a single session resolved the contradiction identically, taking the two specific clauses over the one general one, and each committed its landing record. That is a defensible reading rather than a lucky one, and five identical resolutions is what identifies the defect as the brief's rather than the workers'. The coordinator that supervised them recorded all five as scope violations and held four commits unmerged; that correction is now written beside those ledger rows. The lead has ruled that the fence is the correct half and the worker is always best placed to author both records, because it holds the context that produced them.

The prohibition is not simply wrong. It protects something real — a worker must still not mutate the shared project index, sprint state, or a plan other than the one it is landing against. It is being narrowed rather than deleted in the runtime prompt, and this plan carries the same narrowing into the prose a coordinator reads.

§3 — What the coordinator does instead

The coordinator half is not merely the absence of the old rule. Under the lead's model it reads the worker's committed edits, merges them, and may edit or append to the worker's comments — so the landing beat becomes a review of an authored record rather than a transcription of a manifest. The skill currently describes the transcription.

Two limits belong with it, and both are about what a worker can see rather than about merge mechanics. A worker must not resolve its own driving followup and must not set a terminal status, because only the coordinator observes the other nodes: a worker knows its node landed, not whether the section closed. Those two sentences are owed to the runtime prompt as well, and are recorded here so the two audiences receive the same rule.

Done when the skill's landing-beat description says the coordinator reads, merges, and may append, and states both limits; and no sentence in the skill instructs the coordinator to write a record the worker is required to author.

§4 — Out of scope

The composed runtime prompt is owned by crew-observability-truth and changed by the session holding reckon/crew/prompts.py. This plan mirrors that wording and does not author it; where the skill needs a sentence the prompt lacks, the sentence is added to the prompt rather than carried only by the skill, so no rule lives somewhere its subject does not read.

One distinction is deferred rather than settled here. A worker told to update plan state can reach for the plan MCP, which writes the coordinator's checkout and not the worker's tree — two different stores that the contract text does not currently distinguish. A worker raised this before either coordinator noticed it. The sentence resolving it belongs to the runtime prompt and will be mirrored here in one follow-up edit once its final text lands.
