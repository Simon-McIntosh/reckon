§1 — Why this exists

The served surface was reviewed twice, from two directions, and both reviews reached the same building from opposite sides. The legibility audit asked what the SPA fails to say, ranked fifteen information defects, and closed by refusing to recommend a redesign: “A separate navigation or visual-language proposal is not justified by this evidence.” The plan carrying it drew the same boundary in its own scope — “No redesign … A proposal to restructure navigation or restyle the shell is a separate plan.” This is that separate plan, and what justifies it is not the audit's evidence but the lead's, gathered by using the thing.

Seven surfaces were named as degraded, each with the complaint that named it, and each redrawn on a design canvas that is now imported at design/reckon-spa-redesign.dc.html beside the full interactive prototype at design/reckon-spa-prototype.dc.html. The complaints are the requirement; the canvas is one answer to them.

SurfaceThe complaint, verbatimWhat the canvas answers with
In-flight band “The inflight bar is useful, but could be designed in a cleaner way — does the user need to know the exact worker hash?” Identity by role, not by hash: harness, model, role · effort, member, section, then an elapsed-against-budget bar and the current phase.
Plans list “The dropdown is overly verbose… I typically have it set to just created and rarely edited. It would be useful to see an edited timestamp on all plan interactions.” · “I don't find myself using the left hand scroll bar — an indication that the design could be better.” Two segmented buttons and a direction toggle replace the dropdown; every row carries its edited stamp; the scrolling filter rail becomes a single row of status and sprint chips carrying their own counts.

A prior-art scout read both canvases against all eleven SPA components before any node was cut. Its verdict is unanimous and is the constraint every section below inherits: eight of eight surfaces extend existing implementations; none replaces one. state-loader.js stays the normalised state boundary, shell.jsx:App stays the routing and cross-surface state owner, and plan.jsx:Plan, sprint.jsx:Sprint, crew.jsx:CrewView and the graph.jsx relationship machinery stay the semantic authorities for their surfaces. Styling work may refactor a render tree; it may not fork a data path, add a second loader, or revive the unused legacy cockpit.jsx:Cockpit, which is not a reuse target.

§2 — The redraw must not lose what the audit repaired

A design canvas is drawn from fixtures, and fixtures are always in their good state. That is the defect class this section exists to prevent: every degraded, contradictory, stale and partial state the audit found is invisible on a canvas whose data is hand-made and correct. The scout checked all nine outstanding ranks against the canvas and found four it contradicts outright and two it only half answers. Implementing the canvas literally would therefore re-open defects that were ranked, evidenced and in one case already scheduled for repair.

These are not a second workstream. Each remedy lives in exactly the rows the canvas redraws, so it lands in the node that redraws them or it does not land at all. The rank column below is the audit's own ordering, retained so the finding stays traceable to its evidence.

§3 — The state boundary learns what it currently discards

Three of the four contradictions are the same defect wearing three faces: the loader receives a distinction the server took trouble to make and flattens it before any component can render it. No amount of redrawing fixes that downstream, so this section runs first and alone, and every surface section consumes what it produces.

Deliverables. In docs/ui/state-loader.js only:

  • Snapshot identity survives the load. Preserve resource_versions and source_format from the discovery envelope and stamp a client loaded_at. Discovery emits twelve resource tokens today and the shell discards every one.
  • Every active sprint survives the load. Expose the full set of sprints whose status is active alongside the legacy active_sprint_id, and a derived flag that is true when they disagree. Three sprints are active right now while the legacy index names one.
  • Authored status survives beside effective status. The loader deliberately overwrites the authored value with effective_status; keep both so a compact row can render the transition and its open-gate count.
  • Attachments are typed, not edges. Expose informs, evidence_for and verifies as a typed attachment relation the rail in §5 can group, distinct from the dependency relations the graph consumes.

Done when a test asserts each of the four survives a load of a payload that carries it, and the existing loader tests still pass unchanged.

§4 — Shell: one routing owner, chips you can manage, a visible snapshot

Five of the surfaces the canvas redraws live inside one 1,544-line file. That is a scheduling fact before it is an aesthetic one, and the obvious answer — split shell.jsx into five modules so five workers can redraw five surfaces at once — was checked against the tree and rejected. The asset list that loads the SPA is written out in four places: docs/index.html, the served fallback template in reckon/serve.py, and both templates in reckon/cli.py. All four agree today, and nothing keeps them agreeing. A module split would put five new entries through that seam for parallelism that is available for free by giving shell.jsx one worker for the whole redraw, running its three surfaces in sequence on one retained session.

What the surfaces genuinely cannot share is a stylesheet. Every surface's rules currently sit interleaved in styles.css and styles-base.css, so any two concurrent surface workers collide there and nowhere else. One stylesheet per surface removes the collision without touching a component, and it goes through the four-way seam once rather than once per section.

Deliverables.

  • One stylesheet per surface. Seven files — topbar, plans, reader, overview, sprints, crew, graph — registered in all four asset lists at once, with a test that fails when the four stop agreeing. They start empty; each surface section fills its own, so no two concurrent workers touch one file.
  • The topbar as the canvas draws it. Project chips carrying a live dot, a Manage disclosure, and the tab row. Chips replace the picker; the picker's behaviour does not.
  • Project visibility, without a hard-coded list. The canvas hides nine of twelve projects by naming them, which reckon's repo-agnostic rule forbids. Default to every mounted project visible, persist hiding per browser, and keep the canvas's three rules: a project with no mounted plan state cannot be shown, hiding the focused project hands focus to one that survives, and the last visible project cannot be hidden.
  • The snapshot receipt (rank 10). One quiet shell line naming the source format, the resource count and the load time, with a manual refresh. A live dot without a verifiable receipt is the thing being fixed, so the dot does not ship without the line.

Done when the suite passes with no behaviour change from the split, the static build still bundles every module, hiding the focused project moves focus, the last visible project refuses to hide, and the snapshot line renders the real resource count.

§5 — Plans: chips instead of a rail, and an evidence rail beside the reader

Two complaints meet here. The filter rail is not used, and evidence documents are interleaved with plans although — as the complaint puts it — “an evidence doc is not a plan” and they neither block nor enable anything. The canvas answers both at once: the rail's function compresses into chips carrying their own counts, and the space that frees becomes where attachments live, beside the reader rather than inside the list.

Deliverables.

  • Chip filters. Status chips and sprint chips in one row, each with its count, replacing the scrolling filter column. The existing filter, sort and selection state keeps its authority; only its presentation compresses.
  • A list of plans, sorted plainly. Segmented Created and Edited buttons plus a direction toggle replace the dropdown, and every row carries its edited stamp — both asked for by name.
  • The attachment rail. Research and evidence leave the plan list and become a rail beside the reader, grouped by type, each entry selectable into the same reader. They are attachments, never dependency edges.
  • Rank 6 in the rows. Where authored and effective status differ, the row renders the transition with its open-gate count and links to the gate section. Plain blocked is reserved for when the two agree.
  • Rank 14 in the signals. Every compact mark — percentage, gate count, decision and blocker glyph — gets a label or a tooltip and a navigation target.

Done when the list holds plans only, attachments are reachable only through the rail, a plan whose authored and effective status differ renders both with its gate count, and no compact signal is unlabelled.

§6 — The reader: a band without a hash, honest partial state, a handoff with a receipt

Deliverables.

  • The in-flight band, redrawn. The run hash goes. Identity becomes the thing a reader can act on: harness, model, role · effort, member, section, then elapsed against budget as a bar, the phase, and one action. The run identifier stays available where it is needed — a command to copy — rather than displayed as a name.
  • Partial state stops looking complete (rank 9). When the authored HTML or the structured-state fetch fails, a persistent banner above the fallback names the failed source, the sections that are therefore missing, the response status, and a retry. This is the one requirement with no canvas to copy, because a canvas has no failures.
  • The handoff carries its receipt (rank 13). The copied line is built from live plan HTML and project discovery, and says so, with the loaded plan version beside it so its provenance is checkable.

Done when a failed HTML fetch and a failed state fetch each render the banner with their status, the band renders no run hash, and the handoff shows the version it was built from.

§7 — Reading mode and the palette

The canvas adds a focus mode over the reader: f enters, Escape leaves without unwinding the selection underneath it, and the arrow keys page through the filtered list — the rail is the reading queue, so reading order is list order. The palette gains typed results across projects. Neither is a new engine: the existing keyboard lifecycle, selection routing and reader are what compose into both.

Done when f toggles both ways, Escape always leaves focus mode and never clears the selection, paging follows the filtered order across both the plan list and the attachment rail, and rank 9's provenance banner is visible in focus mode too.

§8 — Overview: every active sprint, and blockers with a next action

Deliverables. Fleet statistics, a project roll-up with per-project active sprint, plan, live and held counts, and the blocker area the canvas already draws — extended per rank 15 with the gated-plan count, and showing unresolved rows only.

Rank 3 lands here, against the canvas. The overview enumerates every active sprint rather than choosing one, labels whichever the legacy index names as the focus, and warns when they disagree, linking the conflicting resources. The canvas's singular find is exactly the defect and is not implemented.

Done when a payload with three active sprints renders three, the legacy focus is labelled as such, the conflict warning renders and links, and unresolved blockers show summary, owner, gated count and next action while resolved rows stay out.

§9 — Sprints: an overview you do not scroll, cards that say one thing

Deliverables.

  • The overview. All sprints on one shared time axis with a horizon selector, sprints holding nothing open folded into a single band with a count, and a legend. Stepping through sprints with ‹ and › stops being the only way to see them.
  • Cards that say one thing. Description, progress, flag. The item contract does not leave the system: why_now and done_when move behind a disclosure, so the card is brief and the contract stays one interaction away.
  • The board, demoted and kept. The existing board becomes the second tab with its data hydration and interactions intact, plus the canvas's Needs-you panel listing the sprint's own open decisions.
  • Ranks 3, 6 and 14 in the board. Multiple active sprints are shown rather than resolved; cards render authored beside effective status where they differ; every compact mark is labelled.

Done when the overview renders every sprint without horizontal paging, folding hides only sprints with nothing open and reports how many, a card body is one description line with the contract reachable behind a disclosure, and the board keeps its existing behaviour.

§10 — Crew: live runs as cards with a measured gate

Deliverables. Rows become cards: identity and phase on one line; the repository, plan and section on the next; budget as an elapsed-against-total bar; gate progress as marks with the current gate named; and the session, host and attach command behind an expansion with a copy action. Per rank 12 the completion contract stops being printed verbatim — cells ranged from 189 to 1,346 characters — and is relabelled to what it is, a done-when, collapsed to one line, with the gate column carrying a measured verdict when one exists. The three-second poll, the elapsed and activity calculations and the error lifecycle are kept exactly as they are.

Done when a run with a 1,300-character contract renders one line, phase and last activity are legible without horizontal pressure, and the attach command copies.

§11 — Graph: a labelled measure and a handle you can ship

The complaint asked for something that already exists: plan-graph-handle and /reckon-ship graph:<handle> ship today, and a bare handle resolves like a sprint id. The surface has simply never shown it. So this section is mostly exposure — the handle, the derived closure membership, the metrics, and a copyable ship line gated by the same open decisions that would hold the ship itself.

Authority is derived, never authored. The canvas is explicit that a repository earns write scope by having a member in the closure, never by being named in a literal. The scope chips render that derivation.

Rank 11 lands here. The current top-level selection is the longest dependency chain by hop count, presented unlabelled as a critical path, while the roadmap already computes a weighted one. Consume the roadmap result, or label the local measure exactly as “longest dependency chain by hop count” and say why it differs. Feeding the old result into the canvas's confident new labels is the one outcome this section forbids.

Done when the handle, its derived membership and its derived authority scope render for a live endpoint, the ship line copies and is disabled while an open decision holds the closure, and no execution-ordering claim is made by a hop-count measure.

§12 — Out of scope

Server, schema and tool surfaces. This plan changes what the browser renders from payloads the server already emits. It adds no endpoint, no plan-schema field and no MCP tool. Where a surface needs something the server does not send, that is a finding to record, not a licence to extend the API here.

The canvas runtime. The imported .dc.html documents are read as specification. Their x-dc template dialect and the generated runtime that interprets them are deliberately not vendored and are not a dependency of anything shipped.

Cold-path latency. A separate measurement this session recorded GET /crew at 46.4 s cold against 1.5 s warm on a four-minute-old server. It is real and it belongs to the latency half of surface-latency-and-legibility, not here.

§ Decisions

How is project visibility decided, given the canvas hides nine of twelve projects with a hard-coded list?

Naming projects in reckon's own source is forbidden by the repo-agnostic rule — project identity comes from the served HTML and from mounts. The canvas's intent is still right: a dozen chips for three projects under development is noise.

A personal view preference does not need a schema field, a write path or a shared decision, and committing one would make every reader of a checkout share one operator's chip row. Deriving it from open work was the near miss: it matches the intent exactly but the chip row would then change under the reader as work opens and closes, which is a worse failure than one stale toggle. Browser persistence keeps the three canvas rules that actually matter — an unmounted project cannot be shown, hiding the focused project hands focus on, and the last visible project cannot be hidden.

Sprint cards were given why_now and done_when by a landed audit remedy, and the lead's complaint is that this is FAR too verbose. Which wins?

Audit rank 5 found that every live sprint item carries both fields and the board rendered neither, so nine sprint resources lost their rationale and completion contract on screen. That remedy landed. The complaint that followed is about the result of it.

The two findings are not actually opposed: rank 5's defect was that the contract was unreachable , not that it was absent from the card face. A disclosure satisfies both — the card is one line as asked, and the contract is one interaction away rather than lost. Dropping it would re-open rank 5 against its own evidence; keeping it on the face preserves a card size the lead has already rejected as hiding other information.

Do audit ranks 3, 6 and 9–15 stay on surface-latency-and-legibility §3, or move to the plan that redraws the same rows?

Both plans are active and both write docs/ui/ . The prior-art scout mapped every outstanding rank to the canvas surface that would carry its remedy, and no rank mapped anywhere else.

A remedy and a redraw competing for the same row are one piece of work, not two, and scheduling them as two would mean either serialising every surface behind the audit's plan or letting two workers write one file. Moving them also makes the contradiction visible: four of the nine are things the canvas would otherwise undo, which only reads as a requirement when both live on the same page.
